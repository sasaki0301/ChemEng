Chapter 3 Summary

Periodic Table and Chemical Bonding

Chapter Overview

In this chapter, we explored how the periodic table organizes elements according to their atomic structure and recurring properties. We examined periodic trends, learned how atoms form chemical bonds, and used Lewis structures to visualize how electrons are arranged in molecules. These concepts form the foundation of chemistry because they explain why elements behave differently, how compounds form, and why materials possess specific physical and chemical properties. Understanding periodic trends and chemical bonding allows engineers to predict material performance, select appropriate materials, design new compounds, and develop technologies ranging from semiconductors and batteries to pharmaceuticals and advanced construction materials.

Lesson 1 Recap: Periodic Table – Families and Trends

Organization of the Periodic Table

The periodic table is arranged according to increasing atomic number.

Atomic Number = Number of Protons

Elements are organized into:

  • Periods – Horizontal rows
  • Groups (Families) – Vertical columns

Elements within the same group often have similar chemical properties because they possess similar valence electron configurations.

Classification of Elements

Category Characteristics
Metals Good conductors, malleable, ductile, usually lose electrons
Nonmetals Poor conductors, often gain electrons
Metalloids Intermediate properties, often semiconductors

Important Element Families

Group Family Characteristics
1 Alkali Metals Highly reactive metals
2 Alkaline Earth Metals Reactive metals
3–12 Transition Metals Strong, conductive metals
17 Halogens Highly reactive nonmetals
18 Noble Gases Very stable and unreactive

Periodic Trends

Property Across a Period Down a Group
Atomic Radius Decreases Increases
Ionization Energy Increases Decreases
Electron Affinity Generally Increases Generally Decreases
Electronegativity Increases Decreases
Largest atoms are found near the lower-left corner of the periodic table, while the most electronegative elements are found near the upper-right corner.

Lesson 2 Recap: Chemical Bonding – Ionic and Covalent Bonds

Why Atoms Form Bonds

Atoms form chemical bonds to achieve more stable electron arrangements.

This often involves achieving a noble gas electron configuration.

Valence Electrons

Valence electrons are the outermost electrons and are responsible for:

  • Chemical bonding
  • Chemical reactivity
  • Electrical behavior

The Octet Rule

Many atoms become stable when they possess:

8 Valence Electrons

Atoms achieve this by gaining, losing, or sharing electrons.

Ionic Bonding

Ionic bonds form through electron transfer.

Typically:

  • Metals lose electrons → Cations
  • Nonmetals gain electrons → Anions
Na → Na⁺ + e⁻
Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻

The attraction between oppositely charged ions creates the ionic bond.

Properties of Ionic Compounds

  • High melting points
  • Brittle solids
  • Conduct electricity when molten or dissolved
  • Crystalline structures

Covalent Bonding

Covalent bonds form when atoms share electrons.

Usually:

Nonmetal + Nonmetal

Properties of Covalent Compounds

  • Lower melting points
  • Poor electrical conductivity
  • Can exist as gases, liquids, or solids
  • Form molecules

Polar vs Nonpolar Covalent Bonds

Type Electron Sharing
Nonpolar Covalent Equal Sharing
Polar Covalent Unequal Sharing
Bond type strongly influences the physical properties of materials.

Lesson 3 Recap: Lewis Structures and the Octet Rule

Lewis Symbols

Lewis symbols represent valence electrons as dots around an element symbol.

They help predict how atoms form bonds.

Lewis Structures

Lewis structures show:

  • Valence electrons
  • Bonding electrons
  • Lone pairs
  • Connectivity of atoms

General Procedure for Drawing Lewis Structures

  1. Count total valence electrons.
  2. Select the central atom.
  3. Connect atoms using single bonds.
  4. Distribute remaining electrons.
  5. Complete octets.
  6. Add multiple bonds if needed.
  7. Verify total electron count.

Types of Covalent Bonds

Bond Type Shared Electron Pairs
Single Bond 1 Pair
Double Bond 2 Pairs
Triple Bond 3 Pairs

Lone Pairs

Lone pairs are valence electrons not involved in bonding.

They influence:

  • Molecular shape
  • Polarity
  • Reactivity

Formal Charge

Formal Charge = Valence Electrons − Nonbonding Electrons − (Bonding Electrons ÷ 2)

Preferred Lewis structures generally have formal charges close to zero.

Resonance

Some molecules require multiple Lewis structures to accurately represent electron distribution.

These are called resonance structures.

Exceptions to the Octet Rule

  • Incomplete Octets (e.g., Boron)
  • Expanded Octets (e.g., Sulfur, Phosphorus)
  • Odd-Electron Species (e.g., NO)
Lewis structures provide the foundation for understanding molecular geometry, polarity, and chemical behavior.

Chapter Review Questions

1. Why do elements within the same group exhibit similar chemical properties?

They possess similar valence electron configurations.

2. Which trend decreases across a period?

Atomic radius generally decreases across a period.

3. What is the fundamental difference between ionic and covalent bonding?

Ionic bonding involves electron transfer, while covalent bonding involves electron sharing.

4. Why are valence electrons important?

Valence electrons determine bonding behavior, chemical reactivity, and many physical properties.

5. What is the purpose of a Lewis structure?

Lewis structures visualize valence electrons, bonding patterns, and lone pairs in molecules.
