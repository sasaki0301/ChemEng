Chapter 3: Periodic Table and Chemical Bonding

Lesson 1: Periodic Table – Families and Trends

Engineering Scenario

Suppose an electrical engineer needs a material for wiring. Copper is selected because it conducts electricity extremely well. A chemical engineer designing corrosion-resistant equipment may choose stainless steel containing chromium. A semiconductor engineer uses silicon to build computer chips. How do engineers know which element is best suited for a particular application? The answer lies in the Periodic Table. The periodic table is much more than a list of elements—it is a powerful tool that allows scientists and engineers to predict the behavior, properties, and reactivity of elements.

After completing this lesson, you should be able to:

  • Identify periods, groups, and major element families.

  • Apply periodic trends to engineering and technological applications.

Why the Periodic Table is Important

Before the periodic table was developed, scientists had difficulty organizing the growing number of discovered elements.

Today, the periodic table allows us to:

  • Predict chemical properties
  • Predict bonding behavior
  • Estimate reactivity
  • Understand material properties
  • Select appropriate materials for engineering applications

Figure 1. The periodic table organizes elements according to atomic number and recurring properties.

Organization of the Periodic Table

Elements are arranged according to increasing atomic number.

The atomic number equals the number of protons in the nucleus.

Hydrogen = 1 proton
Helium = 2 protons
Lithium = 3 protons

As atomic number increases, patterns in properties repeat periodically.

The periodic table is arranged by increasing atomic number, not atomic mass.

Periods and Groups

Periods

Horizontal rows are called periods.

There are 7 periods.

Elements in the same period have the same number of occupied energy levels.

Groups

Vertical columns are called groups or families.

Elements within the same group often exhibit similar chemical properties because they have similar valence electron configurations.

Figure 2. Periods run horizontally while groups run vertically.

Major Regions of the Periodic Table

Metals

  • Good conductors of heat and electricity
  • Malleable and ductile
  • Generally shiny
  • Usually lose electrons during reactions

Nonmetals

  • Poor conductors
  • Often brittle when solid
  • May exist as gases, liquids, or solids
  • Often gain electrons during reactions

Metalloids

  • Properties intermediate between metals and nonmetals
  • Often semiconductors

Valence Electrons and Chemical Behavior

Valence electrons are the electrons in the outermost energy level.

These electrons largely determine:

  • Chemical reactivity
  • Bond formation
  • Electrical properties
Group 1 elements possess one valence electron and are highly reactive. Group 18 elements possess complete valence shells and are very stable.

Periodic Trends

Many properties change predictably across the periodic table.

These predictable changes are called periodic trends.

The most important trends are:

  • Atomic Radius
  • Ionization Energy
  • Electron Affinity
  • Electronegativity

Atomic Radius

Atomic radius refers to the size of an atom.

Trend Across a Period

Atomic radius decreases from left to right.

Reason:

  • More protons are added.
  • Nuclear attraction increases.
  • Electrons are pulled closer to the nucleus.

Trend Down a Group

Atomic radius increases down a group.

Reason:

  • Additional energy levels are added.
  • Electrons occupy shells farther from the nucleus.

Figure 5. Atomic radius decreases across a period and increases down a group.

Largest atoms are found toward the bottom-left of the periodic table.

Ionization Energy

Ionization energy is the energy required to remove an electron from an atom.

Trend Across a Period

Ionization energy generally increases from left to right.

Trend Down a Group

Ionization energy generally decreases down a group.

Figure 6. Ionization energy increases toward the upper-right corner of the periodic table.

Small atoms hold electrons more tightly, requiring more energy to remove them.

Electron Affinity

Electron affinity measures the tendency of an atom to gain an electron.

Atoms with high electron affinity strongly attract additional electrons.

Halogens possess some of the highest electron affinities.

Figure 7. Electron affinity generally increases toward the upper-right portion of the periodic table.

Electronegativity

Electronegativity measures an atom's ability to attract shared electrons in a chemical bond.

Fluorine possesses the highest electronegativity.

Trend Across a Period

Electronegativity increases from left to right.

Trend Down a Group

Electronegativity decreases down a group.

Figure 8. Electronegativity increases toward fluorine.

The most electronegative elements are found near the upper-right corner of the periodic table.

Worked Example 1

Problem

Which atom is larger: Sodium (Na) or Chlorine (Cl)?

Solution

Both elements are in Period 3.

Atomic radius decreases from left to right across a period.

Sodium is farther left than chlorine.

Answer:
Sodium has the larger atomic radius.

Worked Example 2

Problem

Which element has higher ionization energy: Magnesium (Mg) or Calcium (Ca)?

Solution

Both belong to Group 2.

Ionization energy decreases down a group.

Magnesium is above calcium.

Answer:
Magnesium has the higher ionization energy.

Worked Example 3

Problem

Which element is more electronegative: Oxygen (O) or Sulfur (S)?

Solution

Both belong to Group 16.

Electronegativity decreases down a group.

Oxygen is above sulfur.

Answer:
Oxygen is more electronegative.

Engineering Applications of Periodic Trends

Why Engineers Care About Periodic Trends
  • Electrical engineers select conductive metals based on electron behavior.
  • Materials engineers choose elements with desired bonding characteristics.
  • Chemical engineers predict reaction behavior using electronegativity.
  • Semiconductor engineers rely on metalloids such as silicon and germanium.
  • Corrosion engineers use periodic trends to predict oxidation behavior.

Figure 9. Periodic trends help engineers select materials and predict performance.

Check Your Understanding

1. Why do elements within the same group exhibit similar chemical properties?

Elements in the same group have similar valence electron configurations.

2. Which trend increases from left to right across a period: atomic radius or ionization energy?

Ionization energy generally increases while atomic radius decreases.

3. Why is fluorine highly electronegative?

Its small size and strong nuclear attraction allow it to attract shared electrons very effectively.

Lesson Summary

  • The periodic table is arranged by increasing atomic number.
  • Periods are horizontal rows; groups are vertical columns.
  • Elements are classified as metals, nonmetals, or metalloids.
  • Elements within a group often have similar chemical behavior.
  • Atomic radius decreases across a period and increases down a group.
  • Ionization energy generally increases across a period and decreases down a group.
  • Electron affinity generally increases toward the upper-right region of the periodic table.
  • Electronegativity increases toward fluorine.
  • Periodic trends help engineers predict material properties and chemical behavior.
