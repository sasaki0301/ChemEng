Chapter 3: Periodic Table and Chemical Bonding

Lesson 3: Lewis Structures and the Octet Rule

Engineering Scenario

Engineers designing pharmaceuticals, batteries, plastics, semiconductors, and advanced materials must understand how atoms are connected within molecules. Before chemists can predict molecular shape, polarity, reactivity, conductivity, or material properties, they first create a representation of how valence electrons are arranged. One of the most useful tools for this purpose is the Lewis Structure. Lewis structures allow scientists and engineers to visualize chemical bonding and predict how atoms interact in compounds.

What are Lewis Structures?

Lewis structures are diagrams that show:

  • Valence electrons
  • Chemical bonds
  • Lone pairs of electrons
  • Atom connectivity

They provide a simple way to visualize how atoms achieve stable electron arrangements.

Figure 1. Lewis structures represent valence electrons and chemical bonds.

Lewis structures focus only on valence electrons because these electrons participate in bonding.

Lewis Symbols

A Lewis symbol represents an atom and its valence electrons.

Dots surrounding the element symbol represent valence electrons.

Examples:

Hydrogen (1 valence electron)
Carbon (4 valence electrons)
Oxygen (6 valence electrons)

Review: The Octet Rule

Most atoms become stable when they achieve eight electrons in their outermost shell.

This tendency is called the Octet Rule.

Atoms achieve octets by:

  • Gaining electrons
  • Losing electrons
  • Sharing electrons

Figure 3. Many atoms form bonds to achieve a stable octet.

Drawing Lewis Structures: General Procedure

  1. Count total valence electrons.
  2. Identify the central atom.
  3. Connect atoms using single bonds.
  4. Distribute remaining electrons.
  5. Complete octets where possible.
  6. Add multiple bonds if necessary.
  7. Verify total electron count.
Always check that the total number of electrons used matches the total valence electrons available.

Single Covalent Bonds

A single bond consists of one shared pair of electrons.

H — H

Each line represents two shared electrons.

Figure 4. A single bond contains one shared electron pair.

Problem

Draw the Lewis structure of H₂.

Step 1: Count valence electrons.

Hydrogen = 1 electron each

1 + 1 = 2 electrons

Step 2: Form one bond.

Double Covalent Bonds

A double bond consists of two shared electron pairs.

O = O

Four electrons are shared.

A triple bond consists of three shared electron pairs.

N ≡ N

Six electrons are shared.

Problem

Draw the Lewis structure of water.

Step 1: Count valence electrons.

Step 3: Form two O-H bonds.

Water contains two bonding pairs and two lone pairs.

Lone Pairs

Not all valence electrons participate in bonding.

Electrons not involved in bonds are called lone pairs.

Lone pairs significantly affect:

  • Molecular shape
  • Polarity
  • Reactivity

Figure 7. Lone pairs influence molecular geometry and properties.

Worked Example 3: Carbon Dioxide (CO₂)

Problem

Draw the Lewis structure of CO₂.

Step 1: Count valence electrons.

C = 4
O = 6 × 2
Total = 16 electrons

Step 2: Place carbon in the center.

Step 3: Create bonds.

Single bonds alone do not satisfy octets.

Step 4: Form double bonds.

O = C = O
Carbon dioxide contains two double bonds.

Formal Charge (Introduction)

Formal charge helps determine the most reasonable Lewis structure.

Formal Charge = Valence Electrons − Nonbonding Electrons − (Bonding Electrons ÷ 2)

Preferred Lewis structures usually have formal charges close to zero.

Resonance Structures

Some molecules cannot be represented accurately by a single Lewis structure.

Instead, multiple valid structures exist.

These are called resonance structures.

Figure 8. Resonance structures represent electron delocalization.

Ozone (O₃) is a common example of resonance.

Exceptions to the Octet Rule

Not all atoms obey the octet rule.

Incomplete Octets

Boron often forms compounds with only 6 valence electrons.

Expanded Octets

Phosphorus and sulfur can accommodate more than 8 valence electrons.

Odd-Electron Species

Nitrogen dioxide (NO) contains an odd number of electrons.

Figure 9. Some atoms do not follow the octet rule.

Lewis Structures and Molecular Properties

Lewis structures help predict:

  • Bonding patterns
  • Molecular shape
  • Polarity
  • Reactivity
  • Material behavior
Lewis structures serve as the foundation for predicting molecular geometry and intermolecular forces.

Engineering Applications of Lewis Structures

Why Engineers Use Lewis Structures
  • Materials engineers analyze bonding in ceramics and polymers.
  • Chemical engineers predict reaction mechanisms.
  • Pharmaceutical engineers design molecular structures.
  • Semiconductor engineers study bonding in silicon materials.
  • Battery engineers investigate electron movement and ion interactions.

Figure 10. Lewis structures help engineers understand and design materials at the molecular level.

Check Your Understanding

1. Why are valence electrons used when drawing Lewis structures?

Valence electrons participate directly in chemical bonding and determine chemical behavior.

2. How many electrons are shared in a double bond?

A double bond contains four shared electrons (two shared pairs).

3. What is a lone pair?

A lone pair is a pair of valence electrons that is not involved in bonding.

4. Why are Lewis structures important in engineering?

Lewis structures help predict bonding, molecular properties, material behavior, and chemical reactivity.

Lesson Summary

  • Lewis structures show valence electrons and bonding relationships.
  • Valence electrons determine chemical behavior.
  • The octet rule explains why many atoms form bonds.
  • Single, double, and triple bonds involve sharing one, two, and three electron pairs, respectively.
  • Lone pairs affect molecular geometry and reactivity.
  • Formal charge helps evaluate Lewis structures.
  • Some molecules exhibit resonance.
  • Certain atoms violate the octet rule.
  • Lewis structures are essential tools in chemistry, materials science, and engineering.
