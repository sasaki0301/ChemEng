Chapter 3: Periodic Table and Chemical Bonding

Lesson 2: Chemical Bonding – Ionic and Covalent Bonds

Engineering Scenario

Why is table salt brittle while copper wire is flexible? Why does diamond rank as one of the hardest known materials while oxygen exists as a gas? Why do semiconductors such as silicon make modern computers possible? The answer lies in chemical bonding. Chemical bonds determine how atoms combine to form compounds and materials. The type of bond present influences strength, conductivity, melting point, hardness, flexibility, and countless other properties that engineers must consider when selecting materials.

Learning Objectives

After completing this lesson, you should be able to:

  • Describe the role of valence electrons in bonding.
  • Apply the octet rule.
  • Differentiate ionic and covalent bonding.
  • Explain how ions form.
  • Predict whether compounds are ionic or covalent.
  • Relate bonding types to engineering material properties.

Why Do Atoms Form Bonds?

Most atoms are not stable by themselves.

Atoms tend to achieve a more stable electron arrangement by gaining, losing, or sharing electrons.

This stable arrangement often resembles the electron configuration of noble gases.

Figure 1. Atoms bond to achieve more stable electron arrangements.

Chemical bonds form because bonded atoms have lower energy and greater stability than isolated atoms.

Valence Electrons

The electrons involved in chemical bonding are called valence electrons.

Valence electrons are located in the outermost energy level of an atom.

Element Electron Configuration Valence Electrons
Hydrogen 1s¹ 1
Carbon 1s² 2s² 2p² 4
Oxygen 1s² 2s² 2p⁴ 6
Neon 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 8
Valence electrons determine how atoms bond and react chemically.

The Octet Rule

Many atoms become stable when they possess eight valence electrons.

This observation is known as the Octet Rule.

Figure 2. Many atoms become stable when they achieve eight valence electrons.

Sodium has 1 valence electron and tends to lose it. Chlorine has 7 valence electrons and tends to gain 1 electron. Both achieve stable octets after electron transfer.

Types of Chemical Bonds

The two major types of bonds covered in this course are:

  • Ionic Bonds
  • Covalent Bonds

Figure 3. Ionic bonds involve electron transfer, while covalent bonds involve electron sharing.

Ions

Before understanding ionic bonding, we must first understand ions.

An ion is an atom that possesses a net electric charge due to the gain or loss of electrons.

Type Charge Electron Change
Cation Positive Loses electrons
Anion Negative Gains electrons
Na → Na⁺ + e⁻ Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻

Ionic Bonding

An ionic bond forms when electrons are transferred from one atom to another.

This usually occurs between:

  • A metal
  • A nonmetal

The resulting oppositely charged ions attract one another through electrostatic forces.

Figure 4. Sodium transfers an electron to chlorine, forming an ionic bond.

Ionic bonds result from the attraction between positive and negative ions.

Worked Example 1

Problem

Determine the ions formed when magnesium reacts with chlorine.

Solution

Magnesium belongs to Group 2 and loses 2 electrons.

Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻

Chlorine belongs to Group 17 and gains 1 electron.

Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻

Two chlorine atoms are required.

MgCl₂
Answer:
Mg²⁺ and 2Cl⁻ combine to form MgCl₂.

Property Explanation

Covalent Bonding

A covalent bond forms when atoms share electrons.

Covalent bonding typically occurs between nonmetals.

Sharing electrons allows both atoms to achieve more stable electron arrangements.

Figure 6. Covalent bonds form through electron sharing.

Example: Hydrogen Molecule

Each hydrogen atom has one electron.

By sharing electrons, both hydrogen atoms achieve a stable arrangement.

H — H
The shared pair of electrons forms the covalent bond.

Example: Water Molecule

Figure 7. Oxygen shares electrons with two hydrogen atoms in water.

Oxygen requires two additional electrons to complete its octet.

Each hydrogen contributes one electron.

The result is H₂O.

Properties of Covalent Compounds

Property Explanation
Lower melting points Weaker intermolecular forces
Poor electrical conductors No mobile ions
Often gases or liquids Individual molecules exist
Can form complex molecules Electron sharing is flexible

Polar and Nonpolar Covalent Bonds

Electrons are not always shared equally.

The degree of sharing depends on electronegativity.

Nonpolar Covalent Bond

Electrons are shared equally.

H₂, O₂, N₂

Polar Covalent Bond

Electrons are shared unequally.

H₂O, HF, NH₃

Figure 8. Unequal sharing of electrons produces polar covalent bonds.

Engineering Applications of Chemical Bonding

Chemical Bonding in Engineering
  • Semiconductors rely on covalent bonding in silicon crystals.
  • Ceramics often contain ionic bonds, giving them high heat resistance.
  • Polymers consist primarily of covalent bonds.
  • Battery technologies depend on ion movement.
  • Construction materials derive strength from chemical bonding.

Figure 9. Understanding bonding helps engineers design stronger and more efficient materials.

Check Your Understanding

1. Why do atoms form chemical bonds?

Atoms form bonds to achieve more stable, lower-energy electron arrangements.

2. What is the main difference between ionic and covalent bonding?

Ionic bonding involves electron transfer, while covalent bonding involves electron sharing.

3. Which type of bond is most common in semiconductor materials such as silicon?

Covalent bonding.

Lesson Summary

  • Atoms form bonds to achieve greater stability.
  • Valence electrons play the primary role in bonding.
  • The octet rule explains why many atoms gain, lose, or share electrons.
  • Ionic bonds involve electron transfer and attraction between ions.
  • Covalent bonds involve electron sharing between atoms.
  • Polar covalent bonds involve unequal electron sharing.
  • Bonding type strongly influences material properties.
  • Understanding chemical bonding is essential in materials science, electronics, energy storage, and engineering design.
