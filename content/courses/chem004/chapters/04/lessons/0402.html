Lesson 2: Writing and Balancing Chemical Equations

Engineering Scenario

Chemical engineers designing fertilizers, environmental engineers treating wastewater, and mechanical engineers optimizing fuel combustion all rely on chemical equations. Before engineers can calculate how much raw material is needed, how much product will be produced, or how much pollution may result from a process, they must first write and balance chemical equations correctly. A balanced equation is the language engineers use to describe chemical change quantitatively.

Learning Objectives

After completing this lesson, you should be able to:
  • Explain the purpose of chemical equations.
  • Identify reactants and products.
  • Interpret symbols used in chemical equations.
  • Apply the Law of Conservation of Mass.
  • Write chemical equations from word equations.
  • Balance simple and complex chemical equations.
  • Recognize common balancing mistakes.
  • Apply balanced equations to engineering situations.

What is a Chemical Equation?

A chemical equation is a symbolic representation of a chemical reaction.

It shows:

  • The substances that react
  • The substances that are produced
  • The relative amounts involved
Example:
2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

This equation shows that hydrogen reacts with oxygen to form water.

The Law of Conservation of Mass

The Law of Conservation of Mass states:

Matter cannot be created or destroyed during a chemical reaction.

This means:

Total Mass of Reactants = Total Mass of Products

The number of atoms of each element must remain the same before and after the reaction.

Figure 2. Atoms are rearranged during reactions but are neither created nor destroyed.

Word Equations

Before writing chemical formulas, reactions are often described using words.

Hydrogen + Oxygen → Water

To perform calculations, we must convert the word equation into a chemical equation.

H₂ + O₂ → H₂O

Notice that this equation is not yet balanced.

What Does Balancing Mean?

Balancing means adjusting coefficients so that the number of atoms of each element is equal on both sides.

Unbalanced:
H₂ + O₂ → H₂O
Oxygen atoms: Reactants = 2 Products = 1 Not balanced.

Rules for Balancing Equations

  1. Never change subscripts.
  2. Only change coefficients.
  3. Balance one element at a time.
  4. Verify all atoms at the end.
Changing subscripts changes the identity of the compound and is therefore incorrect.

Worked Example 1: Water Formation

Problem

Balance:
H₂ + O₂ → H₂O

Step 1: Count atoms.

Element Reactants Products
H 2 2
O 2 1

Step 2: Balance oxygen.

H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

Step 3: Balance hydrogen.

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

Verification

Hydrogen: 4 = 4 Oxygen: 2 = 2
Balanced Equation:
2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

Worked Example 2: Formation of Aluminum Oxide

Problem
Al + O₂ → Al₂O₃

Step 1: Balance aluminum.

2Al₂O₃

Now there are 4 Al atoms on the product side.

4Al + O₂ → 2Al₂O₃

Step 2: Balance oxygen.

Product oxygen:
2 × 3 = 6 O atoms
Reactants require:
3O₂
Final equation:
4Al + 3O₂ → 2Al₂O₃
Balanced Equation:
4Al + 3O₂ → 2Al₂O₃

Worked Example 3: Combustion of Propane

Problem
C₃H₈ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O

Step 1: Balance carbon.

C₃H₈ + O₂ → 3CO₂ + H₂O

Step 2: Balance hydrogen.

C₃H₈ + O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O

Step 3: Balance oxygen.

Products contain: CO₂:
3 × 2 = 6 O
H₂O:
4 × 1 = 4 O
Total:
10 O atoms
Reactants require:
5O₂
Final:
C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O
Balanced Equation:
C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O

Engineering Example: Haber Process

The Haber Process produces ammonia used in fertilizers worldwide.

N₂ + H₂ → NH₃
Balance the equation.

Nitrogen:

N₂ + H₂ → 2NH₃

Hydrogen:

N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃
Balanced Equation:
N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃

Common Balancing Mistakes

Mistake Why It Is Wrong
Changing subscripts Creates a different compound
Ignoring polyatomic ions Produces incorrect atom counts
Not verifying final counts May leave equation unbalanced
Balancing elements randomly Makes balancing more difficult

Engineering Applications

  • Combustion equations are used in engine design.
  • Balanced reactions determine industrial chemical production rates.
  • Environmental engineers calculate pollutant emissions.
  • Chemical engineers use balanced equations for reactor design.
  • Materials engineers predict reactant requirements for manufacturing.

Check Your Understanding

1. Why must chemical equations be balanced?

Because matter is conserved during chemical reactions, requiring equal numbers of atoms on both sides.

2. What can be changed when balancing equations?

Only coefficients may be changed.

3. Why can subscripts not be changed?

Changing subscripts changes the identity of the compound.

4. Balance: N₂ + O₂ → NO

N₂ + O₂ → 2NO

Lesson Summary

  • Chemical equations represent chemical reactions using symbols and formulas.
  • Reactants are converted into products.
  • The Law of Conservation of Mass requires balanced equations.
  • Balancing involves adjusting coefficients, never subscripts.
  • Balanced equations contain equal numbers of each type of atom on both sides.
  • Engineers rely on balanced equations for design, manufacturing, environmental analysis, and process optimization.
