Chapter 6: Gases and Their Behavior

Lesson 2: Gas Laws – Boyle's Law, Charles' Law, Gay-Lussac's Law, and Combined Gas Law

Engineering Scenario

An engineer fills a compressed gas cylinder at a manufacturing plant. During transportation, the cylinder is exposed to intense heat from direct sunlight. Will the pressure inside the cylinder remain the same? Understanding how pressure, volume, and temperature affect gases is essential in engineering applications involving gas storage tanks, refrigeration systems, scuba diving equipment, aircraft cabins, and industrial processing plants. Gas Laws allow engineers and scientists to predict how gases behave when conditions change.

Learning Objectives

  • Explain the relationship between pressure, volume, and temperature.
  • Apply Boyle's Law.
  • Apply Charles' Law.
  • Apply Gay-Lussac's Law.
  • Apply the Combined Gas Law.
  • Solve gas law problems using proper units and conversions.

The Variables That Describe Gases

The behavior of gases is determined by four primary variables:

Symbol Quantity Common Units
P Pressure atm, kPa, mmHg
V Volume L, mL
T Temperature Kelvin (K)
n Amount of Gas mol
For this lesson, the amount of gas (n) remains constant. We focus on how pressure, volume, and temperature influence one another.

Boyle's Law

In 1662, Robert Boyle discovered that pressure and volume are inversely proportional when temperature and amount of gas remain constant.

P1V1 = P2V2
When volume increases, pressure decreases.
When volume decreases, pressure increases.

Figure 1. Compressing a gas decreases volume and increases pressure.

Why Does Boyle's Law Occur?

When gas is compressed:

  • Particles occupy a smaller volume.
  • Collisions with container walls become more frequent.
  • Pressure increases.

When gas expands:

  • Particles have more space.
  • Collisions occur less frequently.
  • Pressure decreases.

Worked Example 1: Boyle's Law

A gas occupies 8.0 L at 2.0 atm. What volume will it occupy if the pressure increases to 4.0 atm?

Step 1: Write Boyle's Law.

P1V1 = P2V2

Step 2: Substitute values.

(2.0)(8.0) = (4.0)V2
16 = 4V2

Step 3: Solve.

V2 = 4.0 L
Answer: 4.0 L

Charles' Law

Jacques Charles discovered that the volume of a gas is directly proportional to its absolute temperature when pressure remains constant.

V1/T1 = V2/T2
Temperature must always be converted to Kelvin.
K = °C + 273.15

Why Does Charles' Law Occur?

When temperature increases:

  • Particles move faster.
  • Particles spread farther apart.
  • Volume increases.

When temperature decreases:

  • Particles move slower.
  • Volume decreases.

Figure 2. Increasing temperature causes gas volume to increase.

Worked Example 2: Charles' Law

A gas occupies 5.0 L at 25°C. What volume will it occupy at 100°C?

Step 1: Convert temperatures to Kelvin.

T1 = 25 + 273.15 = 298.15 K
T2 = 100 + 273.15 = 373.15 K

Step 2: Apply Charles' Law.

5.0 / 298.15 = V2 / 373.15

Step 3: Solve.

V2 = 6.26 L
Answer: 6.26 L

Gay-Lussac's Law

Gay-Lussac found that pressure is directly proportional to absolute temperature when volume remains constant.

P1/T1 = P2/T2
If temperature increases while volume remains constant, pressure increases.

Engineering Connection

This law explains why:

  • Aerosol cans may explode when heated.
  • Gas cylinders have temperature limits.
  • Tire pressure increases on hot days.

Figure 3. Increasing temperature raises pressure inside a fixed-volume container.

Worked Example 3: Gay-Lussac's Law

A gas cylinder contains gas at 100 kPa and 20°C. What pressure will result if the temperature increases to 120°C?

Step 1: Convert temperatures to Kelvin.

T1 = 293.15 K
T2 = 393.15 K

Step 2: Apply Gay-Lussac's Law.

100 / 293.15 = P2 / 393.15

Step 3: Solve.

P2 = 134.1 kPa
Answer: 134.1 kPa

Combined Gas Law

The Combined Gas Law combines Boyle's Law, Charles' Law, and Gay-Lussac's Law into a single equation.

(P1V1)/T1 = (P2V2)/T2

This equation is used when pressure, volume, and temperature all change simultaneously.

Worked Example 4: Combined Gas Law

A gas occupies 4.0 L at 1.2 atm and 25°C. What volume will it occupy at 2.0 atm and 100°C?

Step 1: Convert temperature to Kelvin.

T1 = 298.15 K
T2 = 373.15 K

Step 2: Apply Combined Gas Law.

(1.2)(4.0)/298.15 = (2.0)V2/373.15

Step 3: Solve.

V2 = 3.00 L
Answer: 3.00 L

Check Your Understanding

1. A gas occupies 10.0 L at 1.0 atm. What volume will it occupy at 2.0 atm if temperature remains constant?

Given:

P1 = 1.0 atm
V1 = 10.0 L
P2 = 2.0 atm

Step 1: Use Boyle's Law.

P1V1 = P2V2

Step 2: Substitute values.

(1.0)(10.0) = (2.0)V2
10.0 = 2.0V2

Step 3: Solve.

V2 = 5.0 L

Answer: 5.0 L

2. Why must temperatures be converted to Kelvin in gas law calculations?

Gas laws require absolute temperature measurements. Kelvin begins at absolute zero, making the mathematical relationships valid. Using Celsius can produce incorrect results.

3. A gas occupies 3.0 L at 300 K. What volume will it occupy at 600 K if pressure remains constant?

Given:

V1 = 3.0 L
T1 = 300 K
T2 = 600 K

Step 1: Use Charles' Law.

V1/T1 = V2/T2

Step 2: Substitute values.

3.0/300 = V2/600

Step 3: Solve.

V2 = 6.0 L

Answer: 6.0 L

4. Why does tire pressure increase during hot weather?

According to Gay-Lussac's Law, increasing temperature increases the average kinetic energy of gas particles. The particles collide more forcefully with the tire walls, causing pressure to increase.

5. Which gas law describes the relationship between pressure and volume?

Boyle's Law.
P1V1 = P2V2

6. Which gas law explains the expansion of a hot air balloon?

Charles' Law. As temperature increases, gas volume increases.
V1/T1 = V2/T2

7. A gas cylinder contains gas at 120 kPa and 300 K. What pressure will result if the temperature rises to 450 K while volume remains constant?

Given:

P1 = 120 kPa
T1 = 300 K
T2 = 450 K

Step 1: Use Gay-Lussac's Law.

P1/T1 = P2/T2

Step 2: Substitute values.

120/300 = P2/450

Step 3: Solve.

P2 = 180 kPa

Answer: 180 kPa

8. When should the Combined Gas Law be used?

The Combined Gas Law is used whenever pressure, volume, and temperature change simultaneously.
(P1V1)/T1 = (P2V2)/T2

9. Convert 50°C to Kelvin.

Step 1: Use the temperature conversion formula.

K = °C + 273.15

Step 2: Substitute.

K = 50 + 273.15

Step 3: Solve.

K = 323.15 K

Answer: 323.15 K

10. A gas occupies 8.0 L at 1.0 atm and 300 K. What volume will it occupy at 2.0 atm and 450 K?

Given:

P1 = 1.0 atm
V1 = 8.0 L
T1 = 300 K
P2 = 2.0 atm
T2 = 450 K

Step 1: Use the Combined Gas Law.

(P1V1)/T1 = (P2V2)/T2

Step 2: Substitute values.

(1.0)(8.0)/300 = (2.0)V2/450

Step 3: Solve.

V2 = 6.0 L

Answer: 6.0 L

Lesson Summary

  • Boyle's Law describes the inverse relationship between pressure and volume.
  • Charles' Law describes the direct relationship between volume and temperature.
  • Gay-Lussac's Law describes the direct relationship between pressure and temperature.
  • The Combined Gas Law relates pressure, volume, and temperature simultaneously.
  • Temperature must always be expressed in Kelvin for gas law calculations.
  • Gas laws are essential in engineering applications involving gas storage, transportation, manufacturing, and environmental systems.
