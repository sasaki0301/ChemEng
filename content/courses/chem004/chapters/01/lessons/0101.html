Introduction: What Is Matter?

Matter is anything that has mass and occupies space. Air, water, metals, plastics, soil, food, and living organisms are all examples of matter.

At first glance, matter may seem continuous. A metal spoon looks like one smooth object, and a glass of water looks like one continuous liquid. Chemistry gives a deeper explanation: matter is made of very small particles that are constantly moving.

Examples of Matter

  • Water in a bottle
  • Steel used in bridges
  • Air inside a tire
  • Silicon inside computer chips
  • Wood used in construction

Concept Formula

Matter = Mass + Volume

Mass
The amount of matter in an object.
Volume
The space occupied by the object.
Common misconception

Air is matter even if you cannot see it. It has mass, fills space, and can exert pressure, such as when air inflates a basketball or a tire.

Key Concept: Particle Theory of Matter

The Particle Theory of Matter is a scientific model. A model is a simplified explanation that helps us understand things that are difficult to see directly.

Fundamental idea:

All matter is made up of extremely tiny particles such as atoms, molecules, and ions.

Principle Simple Meaning Why It Matters
All matter is made of particles Every substance consists of tiny building blocks. This explains why substances can combine, separate, dissolve, and react.
Particles are constantly moving Particles never stop moving completely. This helps explain diffusion, evaporation, pressure, and temperature.
There are spaces between particles The amount of empty space depends on the state of matter. This explains why gases are easier to compress than liquids and solids.
Particles attract one another Forces between particles help hold substances together. This explains why solids keep their shape and why liquids stay together as droplets.
Temperature affects particle motion Higher temperature means faster particle motion. This explains expansion, melting, boiling, and changes in pressure.

Evidence for the Particle Nature of Matter

1. Diffusion

Diffusion is the spreading of particles from an area where they are more concentrated to an area where they are less concentrated.

When perfume is sprayed in one corner of a room, people farther away eventually smell it because perfume particles move randomly through the air.

2. Dissolving

When sugar is stirred into water, it seems to disappear. It has not vanished; sugar particles become distributed among water particles.

3. Brownian Motion

Tiny particles suspended in liquid can be seen moving randomly under a microscope. This happens because invisible molecules collide with them from different directions.

Real-Life Connection

The smell of freshly cooked food spreads through a building because gas particles from the food diffuse into the surrounding air.

Solids, Liquids, and Gases

Solids

In solids, particles are packed closely together and held by strong attractive forces. They cannot move freely, but they continuously vibrate around fixed positions.

Examples: steel beams, concrete, glass, and ice.

Liquids

In liquids, particles remain close together but are not fixed in place. They can move and slide past one another, so liquids flow.

Examples: water, gasoline, cooking oil, and mercury.

Gases

Gas particles are far apart and move rapidly in all directions. The attractive forces between particles are weak compared with those in solids and liquids.

Examples: oxygen, nitrogen, carbon dioxide, and helium.

Engineering Application

Compressed-air tools, scuba tanks, and gas cylinders work because gas particles have large spaces between them. Pressure can reduce those spaces and store more gas in a smaller volume.

Temperature and Particle Motion

Temperature is related to the average kinetic energy of particles. In simple terms, hotter particles move faster on average; colder particles move more slowly on average.

Formula Box: Particle Motion and Temperature

Average particle kinetic energy ∝ T(K)

Kinetic energy
Energy of motion.
T(K)
Absolute temperature measured in kelvin.
∝
Means "is proportional to." As kelvin temperature increases, average particle kinetic energy increases.

Daily Life Example

A hot cup of coffee cools because its particles transfer energy to the surrounding air. As energy leaves the coffee, average particle motion decreases.

Phase Changes and Particle Behavior

A phase change occurs when a substance changes state. The particles themselves do not disappear; their energy, spacing, and motion change.

Process Change Particle Explanation Everyday Example
Melting Solid → Liquid Particles gain energy and move more freely. Ice becoming liquid water.
Freezing Liquid → Solid Particles lose energy and become fixed in place. Water becoming ice in a freezer.
Vaporization Liquid → Gas Particles gain enough energy to escape into the gas phase. Boiling water producing steam.
Condensation Gas → Liquid Particles lose energy and move closer together. Water droplets forming on a cold glass.
Sublimation Solid → Gas Particles bypass the liquid state. Dry ice becoming carbon dioxide gas.
Deposition Gas → Solid Particles directly form a solid. Frost forming on a cold surface.

Worked Example 1: Thermal Expansion

Example Problem

A 10.0 m steel rail warms from 25 °C to 45 °C. Estimate how much longer it becomes. Use the coefficient of linear expansion for steel: α = 12 × 10-6 /°C.

Formula Box: Linear Expansion

ΔL = αL0ΔT

ΔL
Change in length.
α
Coefficient of linear expansion.
L0
Original length.
ΔT
Change in temperature.
Given

L0 = 10.0 m, Tinitial = 25 °C, Tfinal = 45 °C, α = 12 × 10-6 /°C.

Required

Find the increase in length, ΔL.

Solution

ΔT = 45 °C - 25 °C = 20 °C

ΔL = (12 × 10-6 /°C)(10.0 m)(20 °C)

ΔL = 0.0024 m

Unit Analysis

The °C units cancel: (1/°C) × m × °C = m. The answer is a length.

Final Answer

The steel rail becomes about 0.0024 m, or 2.4 mm, longer.

Real-Life Connection

Railroad tracks and bridges include expansion gaps because metal particles vibrate more strongly when heated. Without room to expand, rails and bridge parts can bend, buckle, or crack.

Worked Example 2: Compressing Air

Example Problem

Why can air be compressed inside a tank while steel cannot be compressed easily?

Given

Air is a gas. Steel is a solid.

Required

Explain the difference using particle spacing and attractive forces.

Reasoning
  1. Gas particles are far apart and have large spaces between them.
  2. Applying pressure can reduce those spaces.
  3. Steel particles are already packed closely together in fixed positions.
  4. There is very little empty space to reduce in a solid.
Final Answer

Air is compressible because gas particles are far apart. Steel is difficult to compress because its particles are tightly packed.

Common misconception

Compressing a gas does not make its particles smaller. It mainly decreases the empty space between particles.

Real-Life and Engineering Applications

Students and Daily Life

Food aroma spreads in a cafeteria by diffusion. Hot soup cools as its particles transfer energy to the surrounding air.

Sports and Transportation

Tire pressure changes with temperature because gas particle motion changes. Warmer gas particles collide more often and more strongly with the tire walls.

Technology

Smartphone processors depend on materials engineered at the atomic scale. The behavior of particles affects conductivity, heat transfer, and durability.

Engineering Safety

Bridges, pipelines, rails, and concrete structures are designed with particle-level thermal expansion in mind.

Check Your Understanding

1. Why are gases easier to compress than solids?

Gas particles are much farther apart and contain large empty spaces. Compression reduces those empty spaces.

2. What happens to particle motion when temperature increases?

Particle motion becomes faster because average kinetic energy increases.

3. Why does perfume spread throughout a room?

Perfume particles move randomly and diffuse through the air from a region of higher concentration to regions of lower concentration.

Chapter Summary

Key Takeaways

  • Matter is anything that has mass and occupies space.
  • All matter is composed of tiny particles such as atoms, molecules, and ions.
  • Particles are constantly moving, even in solids.
  • Solids, liquids, and gases differ because of particle arrangement, spacing, attraction, and motion.
  • Higher temperature means greater average particle kinetic energy.
  • Diffusion, dissolving, and Brownian motion provide evidence for the particle nature of matter.
  • Engineering systems such as bridges, rails, tires, and compressed-air tanks depend on particle behavior.
