Chapter 1: Matter, Measurement, and the Language of Chemistry

Lesson 2: Classification and Properties of Matter

Engineering Scenario

A chemical engineer receives a sample of wastewater from a manufacturing plant. The sample contains dissolved salts, suspended dirt particles, traces of oil, and various chemicals. Before designing a treatment process, the engineer must answer several questions:
  • Which materials are pure substances?
  • Which materials are mixtures?
  • Can the components be separated physically?
  • What properties can be used to identify each substance?
The ability to classify matter and understand its properties is one of the most important skills in chemistry and engineering.

Learning Objectives

After completing this lesson, you should be able to:
  • Classify matter as pure substances or mixtures.
  • Differentiate elements, compounds, homogeneous mixtures, and heterogeneous mixtures.
  • Distinguish physical properties from chemical properties.
  • Differentiate intensive and extensive properties.
  • Apply classification concepts to real-world engineering situations.

Why Do We Classify Matter?

The world contains millions of different materials. Scientists and engineers organize these materials into categories to better understand their composition, behavior, and possible uses.

Without classification, it would be difficult to:

  • Select construction materials.
  • Design manufacturing processes.
  • Purify chemicals.
  • Treat water and waste.
  • Develop new products.
Classification helps us understand what matter is made of and how it behaves.

The Classification of Matter

Matter can be divided into two broad categories:

  • Pure Substances
  • Mixtures

Figure 2. Classification of matter into pure substances and mixtures.

Matter Subcategories
Pure Substance Element, Compound
Mixture Homogeneous Mixture, Heterogeneous Mixture

Pure Substances

A pure substance has a fixed composition and uniform properties throughout. Every sample of a pure substance contains exactly the same type and proportion of particles.

Examples of Pure Substances
  • Gold (Au)
  • Copper (Cu)
  • Oxygen (O₂)
  • Distilled Water (H₂O)
  • Sodium Chloride (NaCl)

Pure substances can only be classified into two categories:

  • Elements
  • Compounds

Elements

An element is a pure substance composed of only one type of atom. Elements cannot be broken down into simpler substances by ordinary chemical means.

Examples of Elements
  • Hydrogen (H)
  • Oxygen (O)
  • Carbon (C)
  • Iron (Fe)
  • Gold (Au)
  • Silicon (Si)

Figure 3. Representation of an element. All particles shown are identical atoms.

Elements are the basic building blocks of all matter. Everything around us is ultimately made from combinations of elements.

An element contains only one kind of atom.

Compounds

A compound is a pure substance composed of two or more elements chemically combined in fixed proportions.

Examples of Compounds
  • Water (H₂O)
  • Carbon Dioxide (CO₂)
  • Ammonia (NH₃)
  • Sodium Chloride (NaCl)

Figure 4. Representation of a compound. Different atoms are chemically bonded together.

Compounds often have properties that are completely different from the elements that form them.

Example:

Sodium is a highly reactive metal.
Chlorine is a toxic gas.

When chemically combined, they form sodium chloride (table salt), which is safe to consume.
Compounds contain two or more elements chemically combined in fixed ratios.

Mixtures

A mixture consists of two or more substances physically combined.

Unlike compounds:

  • The components are not chemically bonded.
  • The composition may vary.
  • The components retain their original properties.
  • The components can often be separated physically.
Examples of Mixtures
  • Air
  • Milk
  • Concrete
  • Saltwater
  • Soil

Homogeneous Mixtures

A homogeneous mixture has a uniform composition throughout.

The different components cannot be distinguished easily because they are evenly distributed.

Examples
  • Saltwater
  • Air
  • Vinegar
  • Brass
  • Sugar solution

Figure 5. In a homogeneous mixture, particles are distributed uniformly throughout the sample.

Homogeneous mixtures are often called solutions.

Heterogeneous Mixtures

A heterogeneous mixture does not have a uniform composition.

Different parts of the mixture may contain different proportions of substances.

Examples
  • Oil and water
  • Granite
  • Sand and water
  • Concrete
  • Salad

Figure 6. In a heterogeneous mixture, different substances remain visibly distinct.

The composition of a heterogeneous mixture varies from one region to another.

Properties of Matter

In addition to classifying matter by composition, scientists classify matter using its properties.

Properties help us identify substances and predict how they will behave.

Properties are divided into:

  • Physical Properties
  • Chemical Properties

Physical Properties

A physical property can be observed or measured without changing the chemical identity of the substance.

Property Example
Color Copper is reddish-brown
Density Steel is denser than aluminum
Melting Point Ice melts at 0°C
Boiling Point Water boils at 100°C
Electrical Conductivity Copper conducts electricity

Figure 7. Physical properties can be observed without changing a substance into another substance.

Chemical Properties

A chemical property describes the ability of a substance to undergo chemical change.

Examples of Chemical Properties
  • Flammability
  • Rusting tendency
  • Corrosion resistance
  • Reactivity with acids
  • Oxidation behavior

Figure 8. Rusting is a chemical change that reveals the chemical properties of iron.

Chemical properties are observed only when a chemical reaction occurs.

Intensive and Extensive Properties

Intensive Properties

Intensive properties do not depend on the amount of matter present.

Examples:
  • Density
  • Color
  • Boiling Point
  • Melting Point

Extensive Properties

Extensive properties depend on the amount of matter present.

Examples:
  • Mass
  • Volume
  • Length
  • Total Energy

Figure 9. Mass changes with sample size, while density remains constant.

Worked Example 1

Problem

Classify each of the following:
  • Pure Aluminum
  • Carbon Dioxide
  • Air
  • Oil and Water

Solution

  • Aluminum → Element
  • Carbon Dioxide → Compound
  • Air → Homogeneous Mixture
  • Oil and Water → Heterogeneous Mixture
Final Answer:
Element, Compound, Homogeneous Mixture, Heterogeneous Mixture

Worked Example 2

Problem

A material has a density of 2.70 g/cm³ whether a sample contains 10 g or 1000 g. Is density an intensive or extensive property?

Solution

The value remains the same regardless of sample size. Therefore, density does not depend on the amount of matter.

Final Answer:
Density is an intensive property.

Engineering Connection

Material Selection in Engineering

Engineers constantly classify materials before using them.

  • Mechanical engineers compare densities and strengths.
  • Chemical engineers separate mixtures in industrial processes.
  • Environmental engineers identify pollutants in water and air.
  • Computer engineers require highly pure silicon compounds for semiconductor manufacturing.

Understanding the classification and properties of matter helps engineers make informed design decisions.

Figure 10. Engineers select materials based on composition and properties.

Check Your Understanding

1. What is the difference between an element and a compound?

An element contains only one type of atom, while a compound contains two or more elements chemically combined.

2. Why is air considered a homogeneous mixture?

Its components are uniformly distributed throughout the mixture.

3. Is volume intensive or extensive?

Volume is an extensive property because it depends on the amount of matter present.

Lesson Summary

  • Matter is classified as pure substances or mixtures.
  • Pure substances include elements and compounds.
  • Mixtures may be homogeneous or heterogeneous.
  • Physical properties can be observed without changing composition.
  • Chemical properties involve chemical changes and reactions.
  • Intensive properties do not depend on amount, while extensive properties do.
  • Classification and properties help engineers select materials and design processes.
