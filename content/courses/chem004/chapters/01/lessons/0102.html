Chapter 1: Matter, Measurement, and the Language of Chemistry

Lesson 2: Classification and Properties of Matter

Engineering Scenario

Imagine you are an environmental engineer tasked with designing a water treatment plant. The incoming water contains:
  • Dissolved salts
  • Sand and dirt particles
  • Bacteria and microorganisms
  • Various chemical contaminants
Before you can determine how to remove these substances, you must first answer an important question: What kind of matter is present? The classification of matter allows scientists and engineers to identify substances, predict their behavior, and determine the most effective methods for separation, purification, and use.

Learning Objectives

After completing this lesson, you should be able to:
  • Classify matter as pure substances or mixtures.
  • Differentiate elements, compounds, and mixtures.
  • Distinguish between homogeneous and heterogeneous mixtures.
  • Differentiate physical and chemical properties.
  • Differentiate intensive and extensive properties.
  • Apply classification concepts to engineering situations.

Why Classify Matter?

The world contains millions of different materials. To study them effectively, scientists organize matter into categories based on composition and properties.

Classification helps engineers:

  • Select appropriate construction materials.
  • Design chemical processes.
  • Separate useful substances from waste.
  • Develop new products and technologies.
  • Predict how materials will behave under different conditions.
Classification allows us to understand what matter is made of and how it behaves.

The Classification of Matter

Figure 1. General classification of matter.

Matter can be divided into two major categories:

  • Pure Substances
  • Mixtures
Matter Categories
Pure Substance Element or Compound
Mixture Homogeneous or Heterogeneous

Pure Substances

A pure substance has a fixed composition and uniform properties throughout.

Every sample of a pure substance contains the same particles in the same proportion.

Examples of Pure Substances
  • Gold (Au)
  • Copper (Cu)
  • Oxygen (O₂)
  • Distilled Water (H₂O)
  • Sodium Chloride (NaCl)

Pure substances are classified into:

  • Elements
  • Compounds

Elements

An element is a substance that cannot be broken down into simpler substances by ordinary chemical means.

Elements consist of only one type of atom.

Examples of Elements
  • Hydrogen (H)
  • Carbon (C)
  • Iron (Fe)
  • Gold (Au)
  • Silicon (Si)

Currently, scientists have identified more than 100 elements. These elements are organized in the periodic table.

Elements are the fundamental building blocks of all matter.

Compounds

A compound is a pure substance composed of two or more elements chemically combined in fixed proportions.

Examples of Compounds
  • Water (H₂O)
  • Carbon Dioxide (CO₂)
  • Sodium Chloride (NaCl)
  • Ammonia (NH₃)

Unlike mixtures, compounds have chemical bonds connecting their atoms.

The properties of compounds are often very different from the properties of their constituent elements.

Example:
Sodium is a highly reactive metal.
Chlorine is a poisonous gas.

When chemically combined, they form sodium chloride (table salt), which is safe to consume.

Mixtures

A mixture consists of two or more substances physically combined.

Unlike compounds:

  • Components retain their individual properties.
  • Components can exist in varying proportions.
  • Components can be separated physically.
Examples of Mixtures
  • Air
  • Saltwater
  • Soil
  • Milk
  • Concrete

Homogeneous Mixtures

A homogeneous mixture has a uniform composition throughout.

The components are evenly distributed and cannot be distinguished easily.

Examples
  • Saltwater
  • Air
  • Brass
  • Vinegar

Homogeneous mixtures are also called solutions.

Every sample of a homogeneous mixture has the same appearance and composition.

Heterogeneous Mixtures

A heterogeneous mixture does not have a uniform composition.

Different components can often be seen separately.

Examples
  • Oil and water
  • Granite
  • Sand and water
  • Concrete
  • Salad
Different regions of a heterogeneous mixture may have different compositions.

Physical Properties

A physical property can be observed or measured without changing the chemical identity of a substance.

Physical Property Example
Color Gold is yellow
Density Steel is denser than aluminum
Melting Point Ice melts at 0°C
Boiling Point Water boils at 100°C
Electrical Conductivity Copper conducts electricity

Chemical Properties

A chemical property describes the ability of a substance to undergo a chemical change.

Examples
  • Flammability
  • Rusting tendency
  • Reactivity with acids
  • Corrosion behavior
  • Oxidation tendency

Chemical properties can only be observed when a substance undergoes a chemical reaction.

Intensive and Extensive Properties

Intensive Properties

Intensive properties do not depend on the amount of matter present.

Examples:
  • Density
  • Color
  • Boiling Point
  • Melting Point

Extensive Properties

Extensive properties depend on the amount of matter present.

Examples:
  • Mass
  • Volume
  • Length
  • Total Energy

Worked Example 1

Problem:
Classify each of the following as an element, compound, homogeneous mixture, or heterogeneous mixture:
  • Steel
  • Pure Gold
  • Carbon Dioxide
  • Oil and Water

Solution

  • Steel → Homogeneous Mixture (alloy)
  • Pure Gold → Element
  • Carbon Dioxide → Compound
  • Oil and Water → Heterogeneous Mixture
Answer:
Steel = Homogeneous Mixture
Gold = Element
CO₂ = Compound
Oil and Water = Heterogeneous Mixture

Worked Example 2

Problem:
A material has a density of 7.8 g/cm³ regardless of whether a small sample or large sample is used. Is density intensive or extensive?

Solution

The density remains the same regardless of the sample size. Therefore, it does not depend on the amount of matter.

Answer:
Density is an intensive property.

Engineering Application

Why Do Engineers Need Material Classification?

Mechanical engineers select metals based on physical properties such as strength and density.

Chemical engineers separate mixtures in industrial processes.

Environmental engineers classify pollutants to determine treatment methods.

Computer engineers use highly pure silicon compounds to manufacture semiconductor devices.

Understanding the classification and properties of matter is essential in every engineering discipline.

Check Your Understanding

1. What is the difference between an element and a compound?

An element contains only one type of atom, while a compound contains two or more elements chemically combined.

2. Why is air considered a homogeneous mixture?

Its components are evenly distributed throughout the mixture.

3. Is mass intensive or extensive?

Mass is an extensive property because it depends on the amount of matter present.

Lesson Summary

  • Matter is classified as pure substances or mixtures.
  • Pure substances include elements and compounds.
  • Mixtures may be homogeneous or heterogeneous.
  • Physical properties can be observed without changing composition.
  • Chemical properties involve chemical reactions.
  • Intensive properties do not depend on amount, while extensive properties do.
  • Classification helps engineers select materials and design processes.
