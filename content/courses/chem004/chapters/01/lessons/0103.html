Lesson 3: Measurements and Significant Figures

Engineering Scenario

In 1999, NASA lost the Mars Climate Orbiter, a spacecraft worth approximately $125 million. The investigation revealed that one engineering team used English units (pound-force seconds) while another used SI units (newton-seconds). A simple measurement conversion error caused the spacecraft to enter Mars' atmosphere incorrectly and be destroyed. This event highlights a critical lesson: Engineering success depends on accurate measurements and proper calculations.

Why Measurements Matter

Chemistry is a quantitative science. Engineers and scientists do not simply describe things—they measure them.

Consider the following engineering applications:

  • Measuring the thickness of semiconductor wafers.
  • Determining the amount of fuel needed for a rocket.
  • Calculating chemical concentrations in a water treatment plant.
  • Measuring structural dimensions of a bridge.
  • Determining battery capacity in electric vehicles.

Even small measurement errors can lead to large engineering failures.

Measurements provide the numerical information needed to make scientific and engineering decisions.

Accuracy and Precision

These two terms are often confused, but they mean different things.

Accuracy

Accuracy refers to how close a measurement is to the true or accepted value.

Precision

Precision refers to how close repeated measurements are to one another.

Figure 3. Comparison of accuracy and precision.

Situation Accurate? Precise?
Measurements near true value and close together Yes Yes
Measurements close together but wrong No Yes
Measurements scattered but average is correct Yes No
Measurements scattered and incorrect No No

Uncertainty in Measurements

No measurement is perfectly exact.

Every measuring device has limitations.

Example

A ruler marked every 1 mm cannot measure with infinite precision. If a length falls between two markings, an estimate must be made.
Every measurement contains some degree of uncertainty.

Dimensional Analysis (Unit Conversion)

Dimensional analysis is a method used to convert between units using conversion factors.

Conversion Factor = 1

A conversion factor is a ratio of equivalent quantities.

1 m = 100 cm

Therefore:

100 cm / 1 m

and

1 m / 100 cm

are both valid conversion factors.

Worked Example 1: Unit Conversion

Problem

Convert 2.50 meters to centimeters.

Given:

2.50 m

Conversion Factor:

100 cm / 1 m

Solution:

2.50 m × (100 cm / 1 m)
= 250 cm
Final Answer:
250 cm

Significant Figures

Significant figures indicate the precision of a measurement.

They tell us which digits are meaningful and which are uncertain.

The final digit in a measurement is always estimated.

Rules for Significant Figures

Rule Example Sig Figs
All nonzero digits are significant 456 3
Zeros between nonzero digits are significant 1007 4
Leading zeros are not significant 0.0045 2
Trailing zeros after decimal are significant 5.200 4
Trailing zeros without decimal are ambiguous 1500 Depends

Figure 4. Examples showing how significant figures are counted.

Worked Example 2: Counting Significant Figures

Problem

Determine the number of significant figures in 0.00340

Solution:

  • Leading zeros are not significant.
  • Digits 3 and 4 are significant.
  • The trailing zero after the decimal is significant.

Therefore:

0.00340 → 3 Significant Figures
Final Answer:
3 Significant Figures

Significant Figures in Calculations

Addition and Subtraction Rule

Round to the least number of decimal places.

12.35 + 4.2 = 16.55

Rounded:

16.6

Multiplication and Division Rule

Round to the least number of significant figures.

4.56 × 1.4
= 6.384

Rounded:

6.4

Worked Example 3: Engineering Application

Problem

A machinist measures a metal rod as 12.48 cm and another as 3.2 cm. What is the correct result for their combined length?

Solution:

12.48 + 3.2 = 15.68

The least precise measurement has 1 decimal place.

15.7 cm
Final Answer:
15.7 cm
