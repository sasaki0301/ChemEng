Chapter 5: Stoichiometry and Quantitative Chemistry

Lesson 2: Mole-to-Mole and Mass-to-Mass Stoichiometry

Engineering Scenario

A power plant burns methane to generate electricity. A fertilizer company produces ammonia for agriculture. A cement plant decomposes limestone to manufacture cement. In all of these processes, engineers must answer an important question: "How much product can be produced from a given amount of reactant?" Stoichiometry provides the mathematical framework for answering this question.

Learning Objectives

  • Define stoichiometry.
  • Interpret mole ratios from balanced equations.
  • Perform mole-to-mole calculations.
  • Perform mass-to-mass calculations.
  • Apply stoichiometry to engineering situations.
  • Use a systematic problem-solving approach.

What is Stoichiometry?

Stoichiometry is the study of quantitative relationships in chemical reactions.

It allows chemists and engineers to determine:

  • How much reactant is needed
  • How much product can be produced
  • How efficiently a process operates
  • Material requirements in manufacturing
Stoichiometry is essentially the mathematics of chemical reactions.

Stoichiometric Coefficients

The coefficients in a balanced equation represent mole relationships.

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

This equation means:

  • 2 mol H₂ reacts with 1 mol O₂
  • 2 mol H₂ produces 2 mol H₂O
  • 1 mol O₂ produces 2 mol H₂O

Figure 1. Balanced equations provide mole ratios used in stoichiometric calculations.

Understanding Mole Ratios

A mole ratio comes directly from the coefficients of a balanced equation.

N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃
Mole Ratio Meaning
1 mol N₂ : 3 mol H₂ One mole nitrogen requires three moles hydrogen
1 mol N₂ : 2 mol NH₃ One mole nitrogen produces two moles ammonia
3 mol H₂ : 2 mol NH₃ Three moles hydrogen produce two moles ammonia

The Stoichiometry Roadmap

Mass → Mole → Mole Ratio → Mole → Mass

Nearly every stoichiometry problem follows this roadmap.

  • Convert the given quantity to moles.
  • Use the mole ratio.
  • Convert to the desired unit.

Mole-to-Mole Stoichiometry

Mole-to-mole problems involve converting between moles of different substances using mole ratios.

Worked Example 1

Problem

How many moles of water are produced when 5.0 mol H₂ reacts completely?
2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

Step 1: Write the mole ratio.

2 mol H₂ ──────── 2 mol H₂O

Step 2: Apply ratio.

5.0 mol H₂ × 2 mol H₂O ────────── 2 mol H₂
5.0 mol H₂O
Answer: 5.0 mol H₂O

Worked Example 2

Problem

How many moles of NH₃ can be produced from 4.0 mol N₂?
N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃

Step 1: Write mole ratio.

2 mol NH₃ ──────── 1 mol N₂

Step 2: Convert.

4.0 × 2 = 8.0 mol NH₃
Answer: 8.0 mol NH₃

Worked Example 3

Problem

How many moles of Al₂O₃ are produced from 12 mol Al?
4Al + 3O₂ → 2Al₂O₃
12 mol Al × 2 mol Al₂O₃ ──────────── 4 mol Al
6 mol Al₂O₃
Answer: 6 mol Al₂O₃

Mass-to-Mass Stoichiometry

Most real-world engineering calculations involve mass rather than moles.

Follow these four steps:
  1. Balance the equation.
  2. Convert mass to moles.
  3. Apply mole ratio.
  4. Convert moles back to mass.

Worked Example 4: Magnesium Oxide Production

Given 24.3 g Mg, determine the mass of MgO produced.
2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO

Step 1: Convert Mg to moles.

24.3 g ÷ 24.3 g/mol = 1.00 mol Mg

Step 2: Apply mole ratio.

1.00 mol Mg × 2 mol MgO ───────── 2 mol Mg = 1.00 mol MgO

Step 3: Convert to grams.

1.00 × 40.3 = 40.3 g MgO
Answer: 40.3 g MgO

Worked Example 5: Limestone Decomposition

Given 250 g CaCO₃, determine the mass of CO₂ produced.
CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂

Step 1: Convert to moles.

250 g ÷ 100.09 g/mol = 2.50 mol CaCO₃

Step 2: Mole ratio.

1:1
2.50 mol CO₂

Step 3: Convert to mass.

2.50 × 44.01 = 110.0 g CO₂
Answer: 110.0 g CO₂

Worked Example 6: Ammonia Production

Given 56 g N₂, determine the mass of NH₃ produced.
N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃

Step 1: Convert to moles.

56 ÷ 28.02 = 2.00 mol N₂

Step 2: Mole ratio.

2.00 × 2 = 4.00 mol NH₃

Step 3: Convert to mass.

4.00 × 17.03 = 68.1 g NH₃
Answer: 68.1 g NH₃

Worked Example 7: Engineering Combustion Problem

A power plant burns 32.0 g CH₄. How much O₂ is required?
CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O

Step 1: Convert CH₄ to moles.

32.0 ÷ 16.04 = 1.995 mol CH₄

Step 2: Apply mole ratio.

1.995 × 2 = 3.99 mol O₂

Step 3: Convert to mass.

3.99 × 32.00 = 127.7 g O₂
Answer: 127.7 g O₂

Lesson Summary
