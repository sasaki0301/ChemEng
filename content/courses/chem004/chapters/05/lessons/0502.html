Chapter 5: Stoichiometry and Quantitative Chemistry

Lesson 2: Mole-to-Mole and Mass-to-Mass Stoichiometry

Engineering Scenario

A power plant burns methane to generate electricity. A fertilizer company produces ammonia for agriculture. A cement plant decomposes limestone to manufacture cement.

In all of these processes, engineers must answer an important question: How much product can be produced from a given amount of reactant?

Stoichiometry provides the mathematical framework for answering this question.

Learning Objectives

  • Define stoichiometry.
  • Interpret mole ratios from balanced equations.
  • Perform mole-to-mole calculations.
  • Perform mass-to-mass calculations.
  • Apply stoichiometry to engineering situations.
  • Use a systematic problem-solving approach.

What Is Stoichiometry?

Stoichiometry is the study of quantitative relationships in chemical reactions.

It allows chemists and engineers to determine:

  • How much reactant is needed.
  • How much product can be produced.
  • How efficiently a process operates.
  • Material requirements in manufacturing.
Key idea:

Stoichiometry is essentially the mathematics of chemical reactions.

Stoichiometric Coefficients

The coefficients in a balanced equation represent mole relationships.

2H2 + O2 → 2H2O

This equation means:

  • 2 mol H2 reacts with 1 mol O2.
  • 2 mol H2 produces 2 mol H2O.
  • 1 mol O2 produces 2 mol H2O.

Understanding Mole Ratios

A mole ratio comes directly from the coefficients of a balanced equation.

N2 + 3H2 → 2NH3

Mole Ratio Meaning
1 mol N2 : 3 mol H2 One mole nitrogen requires three moles hydrogen.
1 mol N2 : 2 mol NH3 One mole nitrogen produces two moles ammonia.
3 mol H2 : 2 mol NH3 Three moles hydrogen produce two moles ammonia.

The Stoichiometry Roadmap

Mass → Mole → Mole Ratio → Mole → Mass

Nearly every stoichiometry problem follows this roadmap.

  • Convert the given quantity to moles.
  • Use the mole ratio.
  • Convert to the desired unit.

Mole-to-Mole Stoichiometry

Mole-to-mole problems involve converting between moles of different substances using mole ratios.

Worked Example 1

Problem

How many moles of water are produced when 5.0 mol H2 reacts completely?

2H2 + O2 → 2H2O

Step 1: Write the mole ratio.

Place the wanted substance, H2O, on top and the given substance, H2, on the bottom.

2 mol H2O 2 mol H2

Step 2: Apply the ratio.

5.0 mol H2 × 2 mol H2O 2 mol H2 = 5.0 mol H2O
Answer: 5.0 mol H2O

Worked Example 2

Problem

How many moles of NH3 can be produced from 4.0 mol N2?

N2 + 3H2 → 2NH3

Step 1: Write the mole ratio.

2 mol NH3 1 mol N2

Step 2: Convert moles of N2 to moles of NH3.

4.0 mol N2 × 2 mol NH3 1 mol N2 = 8.0 mol NH3
Answer: 8.0 mol NH3

Worked Example 3

Problem

How many moles of Al2O3 are produced from 12 mol Al?

4Al + 3O2 → 2Al2O3

Solution

12 mol Al × 2 mol Al2O3 4 mol Al = 6 mol Al2O3
Answer: 6 mol Al2O3

Mass-to-Mass Stoichiometry

Most real-world engineering calculations involve mass rather than moles.

Follow these four steps:
  1. Balance the equation.
  2. Convert mass to moles.
  3. Apply the mole ratio.
  4. Convert moles back to mass.

Worked Example 4: Magnesium Oxide Production

Problem

Given 24.3 g Mg, determine the mass of MgO produced.

2Mg + O2 → 2MgO

Step 1: Convert Mg to moles.

24.3 g Mg ÷ 24.3 g/mol = 1.00 mol Mg

Step 2: Apply the mole ratio.

1.00 mol Mg × 2 mol MgO 2 mol Mg = 1.00 mol MgO

Step 3: Convert moles of MgO to grams.

1.00 mol MgO × 40.3 g/mol = 40.3 g MgO
Answer: 40.3 g MgO

Worked Example 5: Limestone Decomposition

Problem

Given 250 g CaCO3, determine the mass of CO2 produced.

CaCO3 → CaO + CO2

Step 1: Convert CaCO3 to moles.

250 g CaCO3 ÷ 100.09 g/mol = 2.50 mol CaCO3

Step 2: Use the 1:1 mole ratio.

2.50 mol CaCO3 × 1 mol CO2 1 mol CaCO3 = 2.50 mol CO2

Step 3: Convert moles of CO2 to grams.

2.50 mol CO2 × 44.01 g/mol = 110.0 g CO2
Answer: 110.0 g CO2

Worked Example 6: Ammonia Production

Problem

Given 56 g N2, determine the mass of NH3 produced.

N2 + 3H2 → 2NH3

Step 1: Convert N2 to moles.

56 g N2 ÷ 28.02 g/mol = 2.00 mol N2

Step 2: Convert moles of N2 to moles of NH3.

2.00 mol N2 × 2 mol NH3 1 mol N2 = 4.00 mol NH3

Step 3: Convert moles of NH3 to grams.

4.00 mol NH3 × 17.03 g/mol = 68.1 g NH3
Answer: 68.1 g NH3

Worked Example 7: Engineering Combustion Problem

Problem

A power plant burns 32.0 g CH4. How much O2 is required?

CH4 + 2O2 → CO2 + 2H2O

Step 1: Convert CH4 to moles.

32.0 g CH4 ÷ 16.04 g/mol = 1.995 mol CH4

Step 2: Apply the mole ratio.

1.995 mol CH4 × 2 mol O2 1 mol CH4 = 3.99 mol O2

Step 3: Convert moles of O2 to grams.

3.99 mol O2 × 32.00 g/mol = 127.7 g O2
Answer: 127.7 g O2

Practice Drills

1. How many moles of H2O are produced from 3.5 mol O2?

2H2 + O2 → 2H2O

  1. Use the coefficient ratio: 1 mol O2 produces 2 mol H2O.
  2. Multiply the given moles by the conversion factor.
3.5 mol O2 × 2 mol H2O 1 mol O2 = 7.0 mol H2O
Answer: 7.0 mol H2O

2. How many moles of CO2 are produced from 5.0 mol CH4?

CH4 + 2O2 → CO2 + 2H2O

  1. The balanced equation shows a 1:1 ratio between CH4 and CO2.
  2. So the moles of CO2 match the moles of CH4.
5.0 mol CH4 × 1 mol CO2 1 mol CH4 = 5.0 mol CO2
Answer: 5.0 mol CO2

3. What mass of MgO is produced from 48.6 g Mg?

2Mg + O2 → 2MgO

  1. Convert Mg to moles: 48.6 g ÷ 24.3 g/mol = 2.00 mol Mg.
  2. The mole ratio Mg:MgO is 2:2, so 2.00 mol Mg produces 2.00 mol MgO.
  3. Convert MgO to mass: 2.00 mol × 40.3 g/mol = 80.6 g MgO.
Answer: 80.6 g MgO

4. What mass of CO2 is produced from 500 g CaCO3?

CaCO3 → CaO + CO2

  1. Convert CaCO3 to moles: 500 g ÷ 100.09 g/mol = 5.00 mol CaCO3.
  2. The mole ratio CaCO3:CO2 is 1:1, so 5.00 mol CO2 is produced.
  3. Convert to grams: 5.00 mol × 44.01 g/mol = 220 g CO2.
Answer: 220 g CO2

5. How many grams of NH3 can be produced from 84 g N2?

N2 + 3H2 → 2NH3

  1. Convert N2 to moles: 84 g ÷ 28.0 g/mol = 3.00 mol N2.
  2. Use the ratio 1 mol N2 : 2 mol NH3, giving 6.00 mol NH3.
  3. Convert to grams: 6.00 mol × 17.03 g/mol = 102.2 g NH3.
Answer: 102.2 g NH3

6. How many grams of O2 are needed to burn 16.0 g CH4?

CH4 + 2O2 → CO2 + 2H2O

  1. Convert CH4 to moles: 16.0 g ÷ 16.04 g/mol = 0.998 mol CH4.
  2. Use the ratio 1 mol CH4 : 2 mol O2, giving 1.995 mol O2.
  3. Convert O2 to grams: 1.995 mol × 32.00 g/mol = 63.8 g O2.
Answer: 63.8 g O2

7. How many moles of Al2O3 are produced from 20 mol Al?

4Al + 3O2 → 2Al2O3

  1. Use the coefficient ratio: 4 mol Al produces 2 mol Al2O3.
  2. Multiply 20 mol Al by 2/4.
20 mol Al × 2 mol Al2O3 4 mol Al = 10 mol Al2O3
Answer: 10 mol Al2O3

8. What mass of H2O is produced from 4.0 mol H2?

2H2 + O2 → 2H2O

  1. The ratio H2:H2O is 2:2, so 4.0 mol H2 produces 4.0 mol H2O.
  2. Convert water to mass: 4.0 mol × 18.02 g/mol = 72.1 g H2O.
Answer: 72.1 g H2O

9. What mass of CaO is produced from 300 g CaCO3?

CaCO3 → CaO + CO2

  1. Convert CaCO3 to moles: 300 g ÷ 100.09 g/mol = 3.00 mol CaCO3.
  2. The ratio CaCO3:CaO is 1:1, so 3.00 mol CaO is produced.
  3. Convert CaO to mass: 3.00 mol × 56.08 g/mol = 168 g CaO.
Answer: 168 g CaO

10. How many grams of CO2 are produced from 44.0 g propane (C3H8)?

C3H8 + 5O2 → 3CO2 + 4H2O

  1. Convert propane to moles: 44.0 g ÷ 44.10 g/mol = 0.998 mol C3H8.
  2. Use the ratio 1 mol C3H8 : 3 mol CO2, giving 2.99 mol CO2.
  3. Convert CO2 to mass: 2.99 mol × 44.01 g/mol = 132 g CO2.
Answer: 132 g CO2

Lesson Summary

  • Stoichiometry studies quantitative relationships in chemical reactions.
  • Balanced equations provide mole ratios.
  • Mole ratios are used to convert between substances.
  • Most stoichiometric problems follow this roadmap:

Mass → Mole → Mole Ratio → Mole → Mass

  • Mass-to-mass stoichiometry requires converting between grams and moles.
  • Stoichiometry is essential for industrial production, environmental engineering, and energy systems.
