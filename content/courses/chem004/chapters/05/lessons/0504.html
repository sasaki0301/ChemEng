Chapter 5 Summary

Stoichiometry and Quantitative Chemistry

Chapter Overview

In this chapter, we explored how chemists and engineers use balanced chemical equations to determine the quantitative relationships between reactants and products. We learned how the mole serves as a bridge between microscopic particles and measurable quantities, how stoichiometric calculations predict material requirements and product formation, and how limiting reactants and percent yield explain why real chemical processes rarely achieve perfect efficiency. These concepts form the foundation of chemical manufacturing, process engineering, environmental analysis, and industrial production.

Lesson 1 Recap: Mole Concept and Molar Mass Calculations

The mole is the fundamental counting unit in chemistry.

1 mol = 6.022 × 1023 particles

This value is known as Avogadro's Number.

Important Relationships

Moles = Particles ÷ Avogadro's Number
Particles = Moles × Avogadro's Number

Molar Mass

Molar mass is the mass of one mole of a substance.

Molar Mass = g/mol

Molar mass is calculated by summing the atomic masses of all atoms in a chemical formula.

Mass-Mole Conversions

Moles = Mass ÷ Molar Mass
Mass = Moles × Molar Mass
The mole allows chemists and engineers to connect atoms, molecules, and ions to measurable quantities such as grams and kilograms.

Lesson 2 Recap: Mole-to-Mole and Mass-to-Mass Stoichiometry

Stoichiometry is the study of quantitative relationships in chemical reactions.

Balanced chemical equations provide mole ratios that relate reactants and products.

Example

2H2 + O2 → 2H2O

Possible mole ratios include:

  • 2 mol H2 : 1 mol O2
  • 2 mol H2 : 2 mol H2O
  • 1 mol O2 : 2 mol H2O

Stoichiometry Roadmap

Mass → Mole → Mole Ratio → Mole → Mass

This roadmap is used for most stoichiometric calculations.

General Problem-Solving Procedure

  1. Balance the chemical equation.
  2. Convert the given quantity to moles.
  3. Apply the mole ratio.
  4. Convert to the desired unit.
Balanced equations act as conversion tools that relate reactants and products.

Lesson 3 Recap: Limiting Reactants and Percent Yield

Limiting Reactant

The limiting reactant is consumed first and determines the maximum amount of product that can form.

Limiting Reactant = Reactant Consumed First

Excess Reactant

The excess reactant remains after the reaction has stopped.

Excess Reactant = Reactant Remaining After Reaction

Theoretical Yield

Theoretical yield is the maximum amount of product predicted by stoichiometric calculations.

Theoretical Yield = Maximum Possible Product

Actual Yield

Actual yield is the amount of product actually obtained from a reaction.

Actual Yield = Product Collected

Percent Yield

Percent Yield = (Actual Yield ÷ Theoretical Yield) × 100

Percent yield measures process efficiency.

Theoretical yield comes from calculations. Actual yield comes from experiments or industrial processes.

Quick Concept Check

1. What number of particles are present in one mole of any substance?

6.022 × 1023 particles

2. What information is obtained directly from the coefficients of a balanced equation?

Mole ratios between reactants and products.

3. Which reactant determines the maximum amount of product that can form?

The limiting reactant.

4. What is the difference between theoretical yield and actual yield?

Theoretical yield is the maximum predicted amount, while actual yield is the amount actually obtained.

5. Why are percent yields usually less than 100%?

Because of incomplete reactions, side reactions, product losses, and equipment limitations.
