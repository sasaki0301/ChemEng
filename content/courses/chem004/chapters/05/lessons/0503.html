Chapter 5: Stoichiometry and Quantitative Chemistry

Lesson 3: Limiting Reactants and Percent Yield

Engineering Scenario

A fertilizer company combines nitrogen and hydrogen to produce ammonia. According to stoichiometric calculations, the plant should produce 1,000 kg of ammonia. However, only 920 kg is collected. What happened? In real-world chemical processes, reactants are rarely present in perfect proportions and reactions rarely achieve 100% efficiency. Engineers must determine which reactant limits production and evaluate how efficiently the process performs. This is where the concepts of limiting reactants and percent yield become essential.

Learning Objectives

  • Define limiting reactants and excess reactants.
  • Determine the limiting reactant in a chemical reaction.
  • Calculate excess reactant remaining.
  • Calculate theoretical yield.
  • Calculate percent yield.
  • Apply limiting reactant concepts to engineering problems.

Why Stoichiometric Predictions Sometimes Fail

In Lesson 2, we assumed that reactants were available in exactly the required proportions.

However, in real chemical reactions:

  • One reactant may run out first.
  • Some products may be lost during collection.
  • Side reactions may occur.
  • Reactions may not proceed to completion.
The reactant that is completely consumed first determines the maximum amount of product that can be formed.

Limiting Reactant

The limiting reactant is the reactant that is consumed first during a reaction.

Once the limiting reactant is exhausted, the reaction stops.

Limiting Reactant = Reactant that limits product formation

Excess Reactant

An excess reactant is a reactant that remains after the reaction is complete.

Excess Reactant = Reactant left over after reaction
Limiting reactants determine product yield while excess reactants remain unused.

Visualizing Limiting Reactants

Figure 1. The ingredient that runs out first limits the number of sandwiches that can be made.

Imagine making sandwiches:

  • 10 slices of bread
  • 4 slices of cheese

Each sandwich requires:

2 Bread + 1 Cheese → 1 Sandwich

Bread could make 5 sandwiches, but cheese can make only 4.

Cheese is the limiting ingredient.

Chemical reactions behave the same way.

Method 1: Determine the Limiting Reactant Using Mole Ratios

Compare the available reactants to the balanced equation requirements.

N2 + 3H2 → 2NH3

Suppose we have:

  • 2 mol N2
  • 4 mol H2

Required hydrogen:

2 mol N2 × 3 = 6 mol H2

Available hydrogen:

4 mol H2
Hydrogen is the limiting reactant because only 4 mol are available while 6 mol are required.

Method 2: Product Comparison Method

Convert each reactant into the amount of product it could produce.

The reactant producing the smaller amount of product is the limiting reactant.

This method is the most reliable and is commonly used in engineering calculations.

Worked Example 1

Determine the limiting reactant.
2H2 + O2 → 2H2O
Given:
  • 8.0 mol H2
  • 3.0 mol O2

Step 1: Calculate water from hydrogen.

8.0 mol H2 × 2 mol H2O 2 mol H2 = 8.0 mol H2O

Step 2: Calculate water from oxygen.

3.0 mol O2 × 2 mol H2O 1 mol O2 = 6.0 mol H2O

Hydrogen could produce 8.0 mol water.

Oxygen could produce only 6.0 mol water.

Limiting Reactant: O2

Worked Example 2

Determine the limiting reactant.
N2 + 3H2 → 2NH3
Given:
  • 5.0 mol N2
  • 12.0 mol H2

From Nitrogen:

5.0 mol N2 × 2 mol NH3 1 mol N2 = 10.0 mol NH3

From Hydrogen:

12.0 mol H2 × 2 mol NH3 3 mol H2 = 8.0 mol NH3
Limiting Reactant: H2

Theoretical Yield

The theoretical yield is the maximum amount of product that can be produced based on stoichiometric calculations.

Theoretical Yield = Maximum Possible Product

Theoretical yield is always determined from the limiting reactant.

Worked Example 3

Determine the theoretical yield of NH3.
N2 + 3H2 → 2NH3
Given:
  • 28.0 g N2
  • 6.0 g H2

Step 1: Convert to moles.

28.0 ÷ 28.02 = 1.00 mol N2
6.0 ÷ 2.02 = 2.97 mol H2

Step 2: Determine limiting reactant.

1.00 mol N2 requires 3.00 mol H2.

Only 2.97 mol H2 is available.

Limiting Reactant = H2

Step 3: Calculate theoretical yield.

2.97 mol H2 × 2 mol NH3 3 mol H2 = 1.98 mol NH3
1.98 × 17.03 = 33.7 g NH3
Theoretical Yield = 33.7 g NH3

Actual Yield

The actual yield is the amount of product actually obtained from an experiment or industrial process.

Actual Yield = Measured Product Obtained

Percent Yield

Percent yield compares actual yield to theoretical yield.

Percent Yield = (Actual Yield ÷ Theoretical Yield) × 100
Percent yield indicates the efficiency of a chemical process.

Worked Example 4

Theoretical Yield = 50.0 g Actual Yield = 42.0 g Calculate the percent yield.
Percent Yield = (42.0 ÷ 50.0) × 100
84.0%
Percent Yield = 84.0%

Why Are Yields Less Than 100%?

  • Incomplete reactions
  • Product losses during collection
  • Side reactions
  • Impure reactants
  • Equipment limitations

Figure 2. Real chemical processes rarely achieve 100% yield.

Engineering Example

A fertilizer plant predicts production of 5000 kg NH3. Actual production = 4600 kg NH3 Determine percent yield.
(4600 ÷ 5000) × 100
92.0%
Plant Efficiency = 92.0%

Practice Drills

1. In the reaction 2H2 + O2 → 2H2O, which reactant is limiting if 4 mol H2 and 1 mol O2 are available?

O2 is limiting because 4 mol H2 requires 2 mol O2.

2. Which reactant is limiting if 2 mol N2 reacts with 4 mol H2?

H2 is limiting because 2 mol N2 requires 6 mol H2.

3. Calculate the percent yield if actual yield is 72 g and theoretical yield is 80 g.

90%

4. Calculate the percent yield if actual yield is 95 g and theoretical yield is 110 g.

86.4%

5. Why is theoretical yield based on the limiting reactant?

Because the limiting reactant is consumed first and determines the maximum product that can form.

6. Name two causes of low percent yield.

Incomplete reactions and product losses during collection.

7. If theoretical yield is 250 g and actual yield is 225 g, calculate percent yield.

90%

8. What does a percent yield of 100% indicate?

Actual yield equals theoretical yield.

Lesson Summary

  • The limiting reactant is consumed first and limits product formation.
  • The excess reactant remains after the reaction is complete.
  • Theoretical yield is the maximum amount of product possible.
  • Actual yield is the amount actually obtained.
  • Percent yield measures process efficiency.
  • Industrial processes rarely achieve 100% yield.
  • Limiting reactant and yield calculations are essential in manufacturing, energy production, and chemical engineering.
