Chapter 5: Stoichiometry and Quantitative Chemistry

Lesson 1: Mole Concept and Molar Mass Calculations

Engineering Scenario

Imagine a fertilizer plant producing ammonia (NH₃). Engineers need to know how many molecules are being produced, how much raw material is required, and how much product can be shipped. However, atoms and molecules are so tiny that counting them individually is impossible. Instead, chemists use a special counting unit called the mole, much like how we use dozens to count eggs. The mole allows engineers and scientists to connect the microscopic world of atoms with the measurable world of grams and kilograms.

Learning Objectives

After completing this lesson, you should be able to:
  • Explain the mole concept.
  • Use Avogadro's Number.
  • Convert between particles and moles.
  • Calculate molar mass.
  • Convert between mass and moles.
  • Solve engineering-related quantitative chemistry problems.

Why Do We Need the Mole?

Atoms and molecules are incredibly small.

A single drop of water contains billions upon billions of molecules.

Counting atoms individually would be impossible.

Just as:
  • 1 dozen = 12 objects
  • 1 ream = 500 sheets of paper
Chemists use:
1 mole = 6.022 × 10²³ particles

This number is called Avogadro's Number.

Figure 1. A mole is a counting unit used for atoms, molecules, and ions.

Avogadro's Number

6.022 × 10²³ particles/mol

One mole of any substance contains exactly:

602,200,000,000,000,000,000,000 particles

These particles may be:

  • Atoms
  • Molecules
  • Ions
  • Formula units
Regardless of the substance, one mole always contains 6.022 × 10²³ particles.

Particle-to-Mole Conversion

Moles = Particles ÷ 6.022 × 10²³

Worked Example 1

Problem

How many moles are present in 1.204 × 10²⁴ molecules of water?

Step 1: Use the conversion formula.

Moles = Particles ÷ 6.022 × 10²³

Step 2: Substitute values.

Moles = 1.204 × 10²⁴ ÷ 6.022 × 10²³

Step 3: Calculate.

Moles = 2.00 mol
Answer:
2.00 mol H₂O

Mole-to-Particle Conversion

Particles = Moles × 6.022 × 10²³

Worked Example 2

Problem

How many molecules are present in 3.50 mol CO₂?

Step 1: Use Avogadro's Number.

Particles = Moles × 6.022 × 10²³

Step 2: Substitute.

Particles = 3.50 × 6.022 × 10²³

Step 3: Calculate.

Particles = 2.11 × 10²⁴ molecules
Answer:
2.11 × 10²⁴ molecules of CO₂

Molar Mass

The molar mass of a substance is the mass of one mole of that substance.

Molar Mass = g/mol

Numerically, the molar mass is equal to the sum of atomic masses from the periodic table.

Molar mass connects the microscopic world of atoms with the measurable world of grams.

Calculating Molar Mass

To determine molar mass:

  1. Identify each element.
  2. Find atomic masses from the periodic table.
  3. Multiply by subscripts.
  4. Add all contributions.

Worked Example 3

Problem

Calculate the molar mass of CO₂.
Element Atomic Mass Quantity Total
C 12.01 1 12.01
O 16.00 2 32.00
12.01 + 32.00 = 44.01 g/mol
Answer:
44.01 g/mol

Worked Example 4

Problem

Calculate the molar mass of CaCO₃.
Element Atomic Mass Quantity Total
Ca 40.08 1 40.08
C 12.01 1 12.01
O 16.00 3 48.00
40.08 + 12.01 + 48.00 = 100.09 g/mol
Answer:
100.09 g/mol

Mass-to-Mole Conversion

Moles = Mass ÷ Molar Mass

Worked Example 5

Problem

How many moles are present in 88.0 g of CO₂?

Step 1: Determine molar mass.

CO₂ = 44.01 g/mol

Step 2: Apply formula.

Moles = 88.0 ÷ 44.01

Step 3: Calculate.

Moles = 2.00 mol
Answer:
2.00 mol CO₂

Mole-to-Mass Conversion

Mass = Moles × Molar Mass

Worked Example 6

Problem

What mass of NH₃ corresponds to 5.00 mol?

Step 1: Calculate molar mass.

NH₃ = 17.03 g/mol

Step 2: Apply formula.

Mass = 5.00 × 17.03

Step 3: Calculate.

Mass = 85.15 g
Answer:
85.15 g NH₃

Engineering Application

A fertilizer company stores 250 g of ammonia (NH₃).

How many molecules are present?

Step 1: Calculate moles.
250 ÷ 17.03 = 14.68 mol
Step 2: Convert to molecules.
14.68 × 6.022 × 10²³
8.84 × 10²⁴ molecules
Engineers frequently use mole calculations to estimate production rates, inventory levels, and material requirements.

Lesson Summary

  • The mole is the fundamental counting unit in chemistry.
  • One mole contains 6.022 × 10²³ particles.
  • Molar mass is the mass of one mole of a substance.
  • Molar mass is obtained from atomic masses on the periodic table.
  • Moles can be converted to particles using Avogadro's Number.
  • Mass and moles are related through molar mass.
  • The mole concept forms the foundation of stoichiometric calculations.
  • Engineers use mole calculations in manufacturing, process design, inventory control, and chemical production.
