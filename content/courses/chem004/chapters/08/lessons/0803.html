Chapter 8 Summary

Solutions and Concentration

Chapter Overview

Solutions are among the most important substances encountered in engineering, science, medicine, manufacturing, and environmental systems. Engineers routinely prepare, analyze, dilute, and monitor solutions to ensure product quality, process efficiency, and public safety. In this chapter, we explored the nature of solutions, concentration units, molarity, dilution, and the practical engineering applications of solution chemistry.

Lesson 1 Recap: Solutions and Concentration Units

What is a Solution?

A solution is a homogeneous mixture consisting of one or more solutes dissolved in a solvent.

Components of a Solution
  • Solute – the substance being dissolved
  • Solvent – the substance that dissolves the solute
Saltwater:
  • Salt = Solute
  • Water = Solvent

Characteristics of Solutions

  • Uniform composition throughout.
  • Single visible phase.
  • Stable over time.
  • Cannot be separated by simple filtration.

Types of Solutions

Solute Solvent Example
Gas Gas Air
Gas Liquid Carbonated beverages
Liquid Liquid Alcohol-water mixtures
Solid Liquid Saltwater
Solid Solid Brass alloy

Solubility

Solubility is the maximum amount of solute that can dissolve in a given amount of solvent under specified conditions.

Types of Solutions Based on Solubility

Type Description
Unsaturated Can still dissolve more solute
Saturated Contains the maximum dissolved solute
Supersaturated Contains more dissolved solute than normally possible

Factors Affecting Solubility

  • Temperature
  • Pressure (important for gases)
  • Nature of solute and solvent
Like Dissolves Like
  • Polar substances dissolve polar substances.
  • Nonpolar substances dissolve nonpolar substances.

Concentration Units

Concentration describes the amount of solute present in a solution.

Mass Percent (% w/w)

% w/w = (Mass of Solute ÷ Mass of Solution) × 100

Commonly used in industrial mixtures and manufacturing.

Volume Percent (% v/v)

% v/v = (Volume of Solute ÷ Volume of Solution) × 100

Commonly used for liquid-liquid solutions.

Mass-Volume Percent (% m/v)

% m/v = (Mass of Solute ÷ Volume of Solution) × 100

Frequently used in medicine and laboratory applications.

Parts Per Million (ppm)

ppm = (Mass of Solute ÷ Mass of Solution) × 106
For dilute aqueous solutions:
1 ppm ≈ 1 mg/L

Parts Per Billion (ppb)

ppb = (Mass of Solute ÷ Mass of Solution) × 109
For dilute aqueous solutions:
1 ppb ≈ 1 μg/L

Lesson 2 Recap: Molarity, Dilution, and Engineering Applications of Solutions

Molarity

Molarity is the number of moles of solute dissolved in one liter of solution.

M = n ÷ V

where:

  • M = molarity (mol/L)
  • n = moles of solute
  • V = volume in liters
Examples:
  • 1.0 M NaCl = 1 mole NaCl per liter
  • 2.0 M HCl = 2 moles HCl per liter
  • 0.50 M KNO₃ = 0.50 mole KNO₃ per liter

Calculating Moles

When concentration and volume are known:

n = MV

This relationship is commonly used in engineering calculations involving solution preparation and chemical dosing.

Preparing Solutions

Engineers frequently prepare solutions with specific concentrations.

General procedure:

  1. Determine required concentration.
  2. Calculate required moles.
  3. Convert moles to mass.
  4. Dissolve the solute.
  5. Adjust to the desired volume.
Accurate solution preparation is critical for process control, safety, and product quality.

Dilution

Dilution decreases concentration by adding solvent while keeping the amount of solute constant.

M1V1 = M2V2

where:

  • M₁ = initial concentration
  • V₁ = initial volume
  • M₂ = final concentration
  • V₂ = final volume
During dilution:
  • Volume increases.
  • Concentration decreases.
  • Moles remain constant.

Engineering Problem-Solving Strategy

  1. Identify the concentration unit required.
  2. Gather known values.
  3. Select the appropriate equation.
  4. Convert units if necessary.
  5. Perform calculations carefully.
  6. Verify that the result is reasonable.
  7. Consider safety and process implications.
Successful engineers not only calculate concentrations correctly but also understand how those concentrations affect system performance and safety.

Quick Concept Check

1. What is the difference between a solute and a solvent?

The solute is the substance being dissolved, while the solvent is the substance that dissolves the solute.

2. Which concentration unit is commonly used for environmental pollutants?

Parts per million (ppm) and parts per billion (ppb).

3. What does a 2.0 M solution mean?

The solution contains 2.0 moles of solute per liter of solution.

4. What remains constant during dilution?

The amount of solute (number of moles) remains constant.

5. Why is accurate solution preparation important in engineering?

Accurate concentrations are essential for safety, quality control, process efficiency, and reliable operation.

Key Takeaways

  • Solutions are homogeneous mixtures consisting of solutes dissolved in solvents.
  • Solubility determines how much solute can dissolve under specific conditions.
  • Common concentration units include % w/w, % v/v, % m/v, ppm, and ppb.
  • Molarity is the most widely used concentration unit in chemistry and engineering.
  • Dilution calculations use the equation M₁V₁ = M₂V₂.
  • Solution chemistry is essential in water treatment, pharmaceuticals, semiconductor manufacturing, and environmental monitoring.
  • Accurate concentration calculations support safe and efficient engineering operations.
