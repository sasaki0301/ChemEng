Chapter 8: Solutions and Concentration

Lesson 1: Solutions and Concentration Units

Engineering Scenario

A water treatment engineer must determine the concentration of chlorine needed to disinfect drinking water. A chemical engineer prepares cleaning solutions used in manufacturing. An environmental engineer monitors pollutant levels in rivers and reports contamination in parts per million (ppm). A semiconductor engineer prepares ultra-pure chemical solutions used in chip fabrication. In all these situations, engineers must understand how substances dissolve, how solution concentration is measured, and how concentration affects system performance. This lesson introduces solutions and the concentration units commonly used in engineering and industry.

Learning Objectives

  • Define solutions, solutes, and solvents.
  • Identify different types of solutions.
  • Explain solubility and factors affecting solubility.

  • Apply concentration concepts to engineering systems.

What is a Solution?

A solution is a homogeneous mixture composed of two or more substances uniformly distributed throughout the mixture.

A solution contains:
  • Solute – the substance being dissolved
  • Solvent – the substance doing the dissolving
Saltwater:
  • Salt = Solute
  • Water = Solvent

Figure 1. A solution forms when a solute dissolves uniformly within a solvent.

Characteristics of Solutions

  • Uniform composition throughout.
  • Components cannot be separated by simple filtration.
  • Particles are extremely small.
  • Appear as a single phase.
  • Remain stable over time.

Solubility

Solubility refers to the maximum amount of solute that can dissolve in a given amount of solvent at a specified temperature.

Solubility answers the question: "How much can dissolve?"

Saturated, Unsaturated, and Supersaturated Solutions

Type Description
Unsaturated Can dissolve more solute
Saturated Contains maximum dissolved solute
Supersaturated Contains more dissolved solute than normally possible

Figure 2. Different levels of dissolved solute produce different types of solutions.

Factors Affecting Solubility

1. Temperature

For most solids:

  • Higher temperature → Higher solubility

For gases:

  • Higher temperature → Lower solubility

2. Pressure

Pressure primarily affects gases.

Higher pressure increases gas solubility.

Carbonated drinks remain fizzy because carbon dioxide is dissolved under high pressure.

3. Nature of Solute and Solvent

"Like dissolves like"
  • Polar dissolves polar
  • Nonpolar dissolves nonpolar

Concentration of Solutions

Concentration indicates the amount of solute present in a given amount of solution.

Concentration answers the question: "How much solute is present?"

Mass Percent (% w/w)

Mass percent expresses the mass of solute relative to the total mass of solution.

% w/w = (Mass of Solute ÷ Mass of Solution) × 100

Worked Example 1

A solution contains 15 g NaCl dissolved in 85 g water. Determine the mass percent.

Given:

Mass of solute = 15 g
Mass of solution = 15 + 85 = 100 g

Step 1: Use the formula.

% w/w = (Mass of Solute ÷ Mass of Solution) × 100

Step 2: Substitute values.

(15 ÷ 100) × 100

Step 3: Solve.

15%
Answer: 15% w/w

Volume Percent (% v/v)

Volume percent is commonly used for liquid-liquid solutions.

% v/v = (Volume of Solute ÷ Volume of Solution) × 100

Worked Example 2

A solution contains 25 mL ethanol in 200 mL solution. Determine the volume percent.

Step 1: Use the formula.

% v/v = (25 ÷ 200) × 100

Step 2: Solve.

12.5%
Answer: 12.5% v/v

Mass-Volume Percent (% m/v)

Mass-volume percent is widely used in medicine and laboratory work.

% m/v = (Mass of Solute ÷ Volume of Solution) × 100

Worked Example 3

A solution contains 8 g glucose in 200 mL solution. Determine the mass-volume percent.

Step 1: Use formula.

% m/v = (8 ÷ 200) × 100

Step 2: Solve.

4%
Answer: 4% m/v

Parts Per Million (ppm)

Very dilute solutions are often expressed using parts per million.

ppm = (Mass of Solute ÷ Mass of Solution) × 106
1 ppm ≈ 1 mg/L (for dilute aqueous solutions)

Engineering Application: Water Quality

Environmental engineers commonly report contaminants in ppm.

Examples:
  • Lead concentration
  • Nitrate concentration
  • Chlorine concentration
  • Heavy metal contamination

Figure 3. Water treatment engineers monitor contaminant concentrations using ppm.

Worked Example 4

A water sample contains 3 mg lead in 1 L water. Determine the concentration in ppm.

Step 1: Apply the ppm relationship.

1 ppm ≈ 1 mg/L

Step 2: Calculate.

3 mg/L = 3 ppm
Answer: 3 ppm

Parts Per Billion (ppb)

Extremely low concentrations are expressed in parts per billion.

ppb = (Mass of Solute ÷ Mass of Solution) × 109
1 ppb ≈ 1 μg/L

Engineering Application: Semiconductor Manufacturing

Microchip fabrication requires extraordinarily pure chemicals.

Contaminants are often measured in ppb or even lower concentrations.

Figure 4. Semiconductor production requires contamination levels measured in ppb.

Comparison of Concentration Units

Unit Common Use
% w/w Industrial mixtures
% v/v Liquid solutions
% m/v Medical and laboratory solutions
ppm Environmental monitoring
ppb Ultra-trace contamination analysis

Check Your Understanding

1. Identify the solute and solvent in sugar water.

Solute = Sugar
Solvent = Water

2. What type of solution is air?

Gas dissolved in gas.

3. Calculate the mass percent of a solution containing 20 g salt in 180 g water.

Given:

Mass of solute = 20 g
Mass of solution = 200 g

Step 1:

% w/w = (20 ÷ 200) × 100

Step 2:

10%

Answer: 10% w/w

4. Why is ppm commonly used in environmental engineering?

Many pollutants exist in very low concentrations, making ppm a convenient unit.

5. Calculate the volume percent of 40 mL ethanol in 500 mL solution.

% v/v = (40 ÷ 500) × 100
8%

Answer: 8% v/v

6. What does the phrase "like dissolves like" mean?

Polar substances tend to dissolve polar substances, while nonpolar substances dissolve nonpolar substances.

7. A sample contains 5 mg/L nitrate. Express the concentration in ppm.

1 mg/L = 1 ppm
5 ppm

Answer: 5 ppm

8. Why are ppb measurements important in semiconductor manufacturing?

Extremely small amounts of contamination can damage microelectronic components.

Lesson Summary

  • A solution is a homogeneous mixture of solute and solvent.
  • Solubility refers to the maximum amount of solute that can dissolve.
  • Temperature, pressure, and chemical nature affect solubility.
  • Concentration describes the amount of solute present in a solution.
  • Common concentration units include % w/w, % v/v, % m/v, ppm, and ppb.
  • ppm and ppb are widely used in environmental and industrial monitoring.
  • Solutions play critical roles in water treatment, manufacturing, medicine, and semiconductor production.
