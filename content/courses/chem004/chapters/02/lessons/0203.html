Lesson 3: Quantum Mechanical Model and Quantum Numbers

Engineering Scenario

Modern technologies such as smartphones, lasers, solar panels, LEDs, and computer processors all depend on controlling the behavior of electrons. Engineers designing semiconductors cannot simply assume that electrons orbit the nucleus like planets orbit the Sun. At the atomic scale, particles behave according to the laws of quantum mechanics. To understand why materials conduct electricity, emit light, or store energy, we must first understand the Quantum Mechanical Model of the atom and the quantum numbers that describe electrons.

Review: The Bohr Model

Bohr proposed that electrons move in fixed circular paths around the nucleus called energy levels.

Figure 1. Bohr's model places electrons in fixed energy levels around the nucleus.

Bohr's model successfully explained the hydrogen atom, but it failed to explain:

  • Atoms with multiple electrons
  • Chemical bonding
  • Electron behavior in complex atoms
  • Atomic spectra of larger elements
Scientists needed a more sophisticated model to explain electron behavior.

The Birth of Quantum Mechanics

In the early 1900s, scientists discovered that matter and energy behave differently at extremely small scales.

Several scientists contributed to the development of quantum theory:

Scientist Contribution
Max Planck Energy is quantized.
Albert Einstein Light behaves as particles called photons.
Louis de Broglie Electrons have wave-like properties.
Werner Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle.
Erwin Schrödinger Wave equation for electrons.

The Wave Nature of Electrons

De Broglie proposed that electrons exhibit both particle-like and wave-like behavior.

Figure 2. Electrons exhibit both particle and wave behavior.

This idea revolutionized atomic theory and became the foundation of quantum mechanics.

Electrons are not simply particles; they also behave like waves.

Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle

Werner Heisenberg proposed that it is impossible to know both the exact position and exact momentum of an electron simultaneously.

The more precisely we know an electron's position, the less precisely we know its momentum.

This means electrons cannot be described as moving in fixed circular orbits.

Figure 3. The exact position and momentum of an electron cannot both be known simultaneously.

The Quantum Mechanical Model

The Quantum Mechanical Model describes electrons as existing within regions of probability rather than fixed paths.

Instead of asking:

"Where is the electron?"

Scientists ask:

"Where is the electron most likely to be found?"

Figure 4. Electrons occupy probability regions called orbitals.

An orbital is a region where there is a high probability of finding an electron.

Energy Levels

Electrons occupy specific energy levels represented by the principal quantum number (n).

Energy Level Value of n
First Shell 1
Second Shell 2
Third Shell 3
Fourth Shell 4

As n increases:

  • The electron is generally farther from the nucleus.
  • The orbital becomes larger.
  • The energy increases.

The Four Quantum Numbers

Every electron in an atom can be described by four quantum numbers.

Quantum Number Symbol Describes
Principal Quantum Number n Main energy level
Angular Momentum Quantum Number l Orbital shape
Magnetic Quantum Number ml Orbital orientation
Spin Quantum Number ms Electron spin

Principal Quantum Number (n)

The principal quantum number identifies the main energy level occupied by an electron.

Possible values:
  • n = 1
  • n = 2
  • n = 3
  • n = 4
Larger values of n correspond to higher energy levels and larger orbitals.

Angular Momentum Quantum Number (l)

The angular momentum quantum number determines the shape of the orbital.

l Value Orbital Type
0 s
1 p
2 d
3 f

Magnetic Quantum Number (ml)

This quantum number specifies the orientation of an orbital in space.

For a p orbital (l = 1):
  • ml = -1
  • ml = 0
  • ml = +1

These correspond to the three p orbitals: px, py, and pz.

Spin Quantum Number (ms)

Electrons behave like tiny magnets and can spin in one of two directions.

+1/2 or -1/2
Only two electrons can occupy the same orbital, and they must have opposite spins.

Worked Example 1

Problem

What orbital type corresponds to l = 2?

Solution

l Orbital
0 s
1 p
2 d
Answer:
d orbital

Worked Example 2

Problem

How many electrons can occupy a p subshell?

Solution

A p subshell contains 3 orbitals.

Each orbital can hold 2 electrons.

3 × 2 = 6 electrons
Answer:
6 electrons

Worked Example 3

Problem

An electron is located in the third energy level. What is its principal quantum number?

Solution

The principal quantum number equals the energy level number.

n = 3
Answer:
n = 3

Lesson Summary

  • The Bohr model could not fully explain electron behavior.
  • Quantum mechanics describes electrons as wave-particle entities.
  • The Uncertainty Principle limits our knowledge of electron position and momentum.
  • Electrons occupy orbitals rather than fixed paths.
  • The four quantum numbers uniquely describe electrons.
  • s, p, d, and f orbitals have different shapes and capacities.
  • Quantum mechanics forms the foundation of modern electronics and advanced engineering technologies.
