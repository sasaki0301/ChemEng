Lesson 1: The Structure of the Atom: Historical Models and Subatomic Particles

Engineering Scenario

Modern smartphones contain billions of transistors packed into tiny microchips. Nuclear power plants generate electricity through atomic processes. Medical imaging equipment uses radioactive isotopes to diagnose diseases. All of these technologies depend on one fundamental idea: Matter is made of atoms. Today, atoms are considered the building blocks of all matter. However, scientists did not always understand what atoms were or how they were structured. The modern atomic model is the result of centuries of observations, experiments, and discoveries. In this lesson, we will explore how our understanding of the atom evolved and examine the fundamental particles that make up every atom.

The Ancient Idea of the Atom

More than 2,000 years ago, the Greek philosopher Democritus proposed that matter was made of tiny, indivisible particles.

He called these particles atomos, meaning "uncuttable" or "indivisible."

Although Democritus had no experimental evidence, his idea introduced the concept of atoms.

Democritus proposed the existence of atoms long before modern science developed experimental methods.

Dalton's Atomic Theory (1803)

The first scientific atomic theory was proposed by English scientist John Dalton.

Figure 2. Dalton's model represented atoms as solid, indivisible spheres.

Dalton's Main Ideas

  • All matter is made of atoms.
  • Atoms of the same element are identical.
  • Atoms cannot be created or destroyed during ordinary chemical reactions.
  • Compounds form when atoms combine in fixed ratios.
  • Chemical reactions rearrange atoms.
Engineering Connection

Dalton's theory helped establish the foundation for modern chemistry, materials science, and chemical engineering.

Thomson's Plum Pudding Model (1897)

Scientists originally believed atoms were indivisible. This changed when J. J. Thomson discovered the electron.

Using cathode ray experiments, Thomson found evidence of negatively charged particles inside atoms.

Figure 3. Thomson proposed that electrons were embedded within a positively charged sphere.

Key Contribution

Thomson discovered the electron, proving that atoms are divisible and contain smaller particles.

Rutherford's Nuclear Model (1911)

Ernest Rutherford performed the famous Gold Foil Experiment.

Figure 4. Rutherford's experiment revealed the existence of a small, dense nucleus.

Observations

  • Most alpha particles passed through the foil.
  • A few were deflected.
  • Very few bounced back.

Conclusions

  • Atoms are mostly empty space.
  • A tiny, dense nucleus exists at the center.
  • The nucleus contains positive charge.
Rutherford's experiment completely changed our understanding of atomic structure.

Bohr's Planetary Model (1913)

Niels Bohr improved Rutherford's model by proposing that electrons move in specific energy levels around the nucleus.

Figure 5. Bohr proposed that electrons occupy fixed energy levels around the nucleus.

Electrons could move between energy levels by absorbing or releasing energy.

Bohr's model successfully explained the emission spectrum of hydrogen.

The Modern Atomic Model

Modern atomic theory combines the discoveries of many scientists.

Today, scientists use the Quantum Mechanical Model, which describes electrons as existing within probability regions called orbitals.

Figure 6. The modern model describes electrons using probability distributions rather than fixed paths.

Unlike Bohr's model, electrons do not travel in fixed circular paths.

Understanding Protons

Protons are positively charged particles found inside the nucleus.

The number of protons determines the identity of an element.

Hydrogen has 1 proton.
Carbon has 6 protons.
Oxygen has 8 protons.
Changing the number of protons changes the element itself.

Understanding Neutrons

Neutrons are neutral particles found in the nucleus.

Neutrons contribute to atomic mass and help stabilize the nucleus.

Carbon-12 contains 6 neutrons.
Carbon-14 contains 8 neutrons.

These forms of carbon are called isotopes and will be discussed in the next lesson.

Understanding Electrons

Electrons are negatively charged particles located outside the nucleus.

Electrons are responsible for:

  • Chemical bonding
  • Electrical conductivity
  • Chemical reactions
  • Material properties
The movement of electrons allows electric current to flow through wires and electronic devices.

Worked Example 1

Problem

Which subatomic particle determines the identity of an element?

Solution

The atomic number is equal to the number of protons. Each element has a unique number of protons.

Answer:
Protons determine the identity of an element.

Worked Example 2

Problem

An atom contains 8 protons. Which element is it?

Solution

Atomic number = number of protons

Atomic number = 8

Element with atomic number 8 = Oxygen

Answer:
The atom is Oxygen.
