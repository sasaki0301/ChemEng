Chapter 2: Atomic Structure and Electronic Configuration

Lesson 2: Isotopes and Average Atomic Mass

Engineering Scenario

Imagine you are working as a nuclear engineer at a power plant. You discover that not all uranium atoms are identical. Some uranium atoms are useful as nuclear fuel, while others are much less likely to undergo nuclear fission. How can atoms of the same element behave differently? The answer lies in the concept of isotopes. Although isotopes belong to the same element and contain the same number of protons, they may contain different numbers of neutrons. This difference affects their mass and, in some cases, their stability and usefulness in engineering applications. Understanding isotopes is important in nuclear energy, medicine, environmental science, archaeology, and materials analysis.

Learning Objectives

After completing this lesson, you should be able to:
  • Define isotopes.
  • Differentiate isotopes of the same element.
  • Interpret isotope notation.
  • Determine the number of protons, neutrons, and electrons in isotopes.
  • Calculate average atomic mass using isotopic abundances.
  • Relate isotopes to engineering and technological applications.

Review: The Structure of an Atom

Recall that atoms contain three major subatomic particles:

Particle Charge Location
Proton +1 Nucleus
Neutron 0 Nucleus
Electron -1 Electron Cloud

The number of protons determines the identity of an element.

Atoms of the same element always have the same number of protons.

What Are Isotopes?

Isotopes are atoms of the same element that contain the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.

Because the number of protons remains the same, isotopes remain the same element.

However, because the number of neutrons differs, their masses are different.

Figure 1. Carbon isotopes have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.

Example: Carbon Isotopes

Isotope Protons Neutrons Mass Number
Carbon-12 6 6 12
Carbon-13 6 7 13
Carbon-14 6 8 14

All three isotopes are carbon because each contains 6 protons.

They differ only in the number of neutrons.

Changing the number of neutrons changes the isotope but not the element.

Mass Number

The mass number represents the total number of protons and neutrons in an atom.

Mass Number = Protons + Neutrons
Carbon-13:
  • Protons = 6
  • Neutrons = 7
Mass Number = 6 + 7 = 13

Atomic Number

The atomic number is the number of protons in an atom.

Atomic Number = Number of Protons

Every element has a unique atomic number.

Element Atomic Number
Hydrogen 1
Carbon 6
Oxygen 8
Sodium 11
Atomic number identifies the element.

Isotope Notation

Scientists use a special notation to represent isotopes.

Figure 2. Standard isotope notation showing atomic number and mass number.

Example:

¹⁴₆C

Where:

  • 14 = Mass Number
  • 6 = Atomic Number
  • C = Carbon

Determining Subatomic Particles

Using isotope notation, we can determine the number of protons, neutrons, and electrons.

Example:

Oxygen-18 Atomic Number = 8 Mass Number = 18

Protons:

8

Neutrons:

18 − 8 = 10

Electrons (neutral atom):

8
For neutral atoms:
  • Protons = Electrons
  • Neutrons = Mass Number − Atomic Number

Why Average Atomic Mass Is Needed

Most elements found in nature consist of multiple isotopes.

For example, naturally occurring chlorine contains:

  • Chlorine-35
  • Chlorine-37

Because samples contain a mixture of isotopes, the atomic masses listed on the periodic table are weighted averages.

Figure 3. Elements exist as mixtures of naturally occurring isotopes.

Average Atomic Mass

The average atomic mass takes into account:

  • The mass of each isotope
  • The abundance of each isotope
Average Atomic Mass = Σ(Mass × Fractional Abundance)

Worked Example 1

Problem

Determine the number of protons, neutrons, and electrons in Carbon-14.

Given:

  • Atomic Number = 6
  • Mass Number = 14

Solution:

Protons:

6

Neutrons:

14 - 6 = 8

Electrons:

6
Answer:
Protons = 6
Neutrons = 8
Electrons = 6

Worked Example 2

Problem

Calculate the average atomic mass of an element with the following isotopes:
  • Isotope A: 20 amu, 75% abundance
  • Isotope B: 22 amu, 25% abundance

Solution:

Average Atomic Mass = (20 × 0.75) + (22 × 0.25)
= 15 + 5.5
= 20.5 amu
Answer:
20.5 amu

Worked Example 3

Problem

Copper consists of:
  • Copper-63 (69%)
  • Copper-65 (31%)
Estimate the average atomic mass.

Solution:

(63 × 0.69) + (65 × 0.31)
= 43.47 + 20.15
= 63.62 amu
Answer:
63.62 amu

Engineering and Real-World Applications of Isotopes

Figure 4. Isotopes are used in medicine, industry, energy production, and scientific research.

Nuclear Energy

Uranium-235 is used as fuel in nuclear reactors.

Medicine

Radioactive isotopes are used in medical imaging and cancer treatment.

Archaeology

Carbon-14 is used to determine the age of ancient artifacts.

Industry

Radioactive tracers help engineers detect leaks in pipelines and industrial systems.

Different isotopes of the same element can have dramatically different applications.

Check Your Understanding

1. What makes two atoms isotopes of the same element?

They have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.

2. How many neutrons are present in Oxygen-18?

10 neutrons.

3. Why is average atomic mass usually not a whole number?

Because it is a weighted average of multiple naturally occurring isotopes.

Lesson Summary

  • Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons.
  • Atomic number equals the number of protons.
  • Mass number equals protons plus neutrons.
  • Changing neutrons changes the isotope but not the element.
  • Isotope notation shows atomic number and mass number.
  • Average atomic mass is a weighted average based on isotopic abundance.
  • Isotopes have important applications in medicine, engineering, industry, and nuclear energy.
